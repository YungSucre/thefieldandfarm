Designing a corn maze starts with a clear goal: is it for family fun, a fundraiser, or a major agritourism draw? Your answer determines size, complexity, and budget. For a first maze, aim for 2 to 3 acres, which is manageable and profitable. This guide walks you through the design process, from choosing a field to cutting paths, with realistic costs and timelines for 2026.

## Choose the right field and corn variety

Your field must be relatively flat, well-drained, and visible from the road. Avoid low spots that flood after summer storms. For the corn, select a tall, sturdy field corn variety, not sweet corn. Field corn grows 8 to 10 feet tall and holds up better to foot traffic. Good choices include 'Pioneer P1197' or 'Dekalb DKC62-08', both widely available from seed dealers. ## Design the maze on paper first

Before planting, sketch your maze on graph paper or use design software like AutoCAD or a simple grid in Excel. Keep the design simple for your first year: a single path with a few dead ends and a central hub. Complex multi-loop mazes are harder to maintain and confusing for visitors. Aim for a path width of 3 feet, which is comfortable for walking and allows for emergency access. Leave a 10-foot perimeter around the maze for a path and to prevent visitors from cutting through the edge. Mark all entrances, exits, and emergency exits clearly on your map.

## Planting and cutting the maze

Plant corn in rows 30 inches apart, which is standard for field corn. After the corn reaches 6 to 8 inches tall, you can mark the maze layout using flags or GPS. For cutting, use a string trimmer or a small tractor with a mower deck. A walk-behind trimmer works for small mazes; for larger ones, a riding mower with a 48-inch deck saves time. Cut paths when the corn is 2 to 3 feet tall, usually late June to early July. This timing allows the corn to recover and grow tall by fall. Cutting too early can stunt growth; cutting too late makes the paths hard to see.

## Add features and navigation

To make the maze more than just a walk, add checkpoints with trivia questions, photo spots, or a scavenger hunt. Use colored flags or painted posts to mark checkpoints. For navigation, provide visitors with a map or a smartphone app that tracks their location. A simple paper map with a "you are here" marker works well. For safety, install emergency exits every 100 feet, marked with a different color flag. Also, create a central "hub" where paths converge, with a staff member stationed there during busy hours.

## Budget and pricing for 2026

Here's a realistic budget for a 2-acre maze:

| Item | Cost |
| --- | --- |
| Seed corn | $300 - $500 |
| Fertilizer and herbicide | $200 - $400 |
| Cutting and maintenance | $100 - $200 |
| Marketing (signs, flyers) | $200 - $500 |
| Insurance (liability) | $500 - $1,000 |
| Total | $1,300 - $2,600 |

Charge admission: $10 to $15 per adult, $8 to $12 for children under 12. A 2-acre maze can host 500 to 1,000 visitors per season, generating $5,000 to $15,000 in revenue. Add revenue with pumpkin sales, hayrides, or a small concession stand. Break-even is typically 200 to 300 visitors.

## Marketing and operations

Start promoting your maze in August, using social media and local event listings. Create a simple website with hours, prices, and a map. Partner with local schools for field trips, which can fill weekday slots. On weekends, consider a "flashlight night" or a "haunted maze" in October to draw older crowds. Train staff on safety and customer service. Have a clear policy for lost visitors: staff should patrol the maze every 30 minutes.

## FAQ

**How long does it take to design and cut a maze?**

Designing on paper takes 1 to 2 days. Cutting a 2-acre maze with a walk-behind trimmer takes 2 to 3 days; with a riding mower, 1 day.

**Can I use sweet corn instead of field corn?**

Sweet corn is shorter and less sturdy, so it's not ideal. Field corn is cheaper and grows taller. Stick with field corn.

**What if my corn doesn't grow tall enough?**

Plant early and ensure good soil fertility. If a drought hits, irrigate if possible. If the corn is stunted, you can still cut a maze, but paths will be less enclosed. Consider adding temporary fencing or hay bales to define paths.

**Do I need a permit to open a corn maze?**

Check local zoning and business regulations. Most rural areas require a simple business license and liability insurance. Some states have agritourism laws that limit liability, but you should still carry insurance.

## The bottom line

Designing a corn maze is a rewarding project that can turn a simple field into a profitable fall attraction. Start small, plan on paper, and cut paths in early summer. With a budget of $1,300 to $2,600 and admission fees of $10 to $15, you can break even with just a few hundred visitors. Focus on a simple design, safe navigation, and a fun experience for families. If you're new to agritourism, a corn maze is a low-cost entry point with high potential. Get your design ready now, and you'll be ready to plant in spring.
