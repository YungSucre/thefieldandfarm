If you're deciding between growing full-size broccoli heads or broccoli sprouts, here's the short answer: broccoli sprouts are faster, cheaper, and more nutrient-dense per bite, but they're not a substitute for the hearty vegetable. Grow both if you have space, but start with sprouts for quick wins and plant broccoli for long-term harvests.

## What's the difference?

Broccoli (Brassica oleracea var. italica) is the mature plant you grow in the garden, taking 60 to 100 days from transplant to harvest a single head. Broccoli sprouts are the young seedlings, harvested 5 to 7 days after germination, eaten whole (stem, cotyledons, and tiny leaves). They're not the same a

## Nutrition: sprouts pack a punch

Broccoli sprouts contain 10 to 100 times more sulforaphane (the compound linked to cancer prevention) than mature broccoli, according to a 1997 Johns Hopkins study. A 3-ounce serving of sprouts has about 20 calories and 2 grams of protein, while a cup of cooked broccoli has 55 calories and 4 grams. For sulforaphane, sprouts win decisively, but mature broccoli provides more fiber and vitamins A and K per serving.

## Growing broccoli in the garden

### Timing and varieties

Broccoli is a cool-season crop. In USDA zones 3 to 6, plant transplants in early spring (4 to 6 weeks before last frost) and again in midsummer for fall harvest. In zones 7 to 9, grow it as a fall crop, planting in August or September. Choose varieties suited to your season: 'Calabrese' (60 days) for spring, 'Belstar' (65 days) for both spring and fall, 'Arcadia' (70 days) for fall, and 'Purple Sprouting' (200 days) for overwintering in mild zones.

### Planting and care

Start seeds indoors 6 weeks before transplant, or buy transplants at a nursery. Space plants 18 inches apart in rows 24 inches apart. Plant in rich, well-drained soil with pH 6.0 to 7.0. Fertilize with a balanced 10-10-10 at planting and side-dress with nitrogen 3 weeks later. Keep soil consistently moist; mulch to retain moisture and cool the roots.

### Harvest and yield

Harvest the central head when it's tight and dark green, before the florets start to separate. Cut 6 inches of stem with a sharp knife. After cutting, side shoots will form, giving you smaller florets for weeks. Expect 1 to 2 pounds per plant in a good season. A 10-foot row of 6 plants yields about 9 pounds total.

### Common problems

Cabbage worms and aphids are the main pests. Cover plants with floating row cover immediately after transplanting, and check undersides of leaves weekly. For aphids, spray with a strong jet of water or insecticidal soap. Avoid planting in the same spot as other brassicas for 3 years to prevent clubroot.

## Growing broccoli sprouts at home

### What you need

You don't need a garden, just a sunny windowsill or a simple sprouting jar. Supplies: a wide-mouth mason jar, a mesh sprouting lid (or cheesecloth and a rubber band), and organic broccoli seeds. A 4-ounce jar of seeds costs $8 to $12 and yields about 2 pounds of sprouts over multiple batches.

### Step-by-step method

1. Measure 2 tablespoons of seeds (about 10 grams) into the jar.
2. Cover with 2 cups of cool water and let soak for 8 to 12 hours.
3. Drain and rinse the seeds through the mesh lid.
4. Invert the jar at a 45-degree angle in a bowl or on a dish rack to allow air circulation and drainage.
5. Rinse and drain the seeds twice a day (morning and evening) for 5 to 7 days.
6. When sprouts are 1 to 2 inches long and the leaves are green (expose them to indirect light the last day for greening), they're ready.
7. Rinse thoroughly, drain well, and store in a sealed container in the fridge for up to 5 days.

### Yield and cost

Two tablespoons of seeds yield about 1.5 cups of sprouts, roughly 3 ounces. At $10 per 4-ounce jar of seeds, that's about $1.25 per batch, far cheaper than the $4 to $6 per 4-ounce clamshell at the grocery store. You can grow a batch every week with minimal effort.

### Safety note

Use seeds specifically labeled for sprouting, as they're tested for pathogens. Rinse thoroughly and keep everything clean. If sprouts smell sour or look slimy, discard them.

## Which should you grow?

If you have a garden and want a staple vegetable, grow broccoli. It's more filling, versatile in cooking, and gives you a substantial harvest. If you're short on space or time, or want a concentrated health boost, grow sprouts. They're ready in a week, take up a jar, and cost pennies per serving. Many gardeners do both: sprouts for immediate nutrition, broccoli for the freezer.

## FAQ

### Can I use regular broccoli seeds for sprouts?

Yes, but choose untreated, organic seeds labeled for sprouting. Regular garden seeds may be treated with fungicides, which are not safe to eat raw.

### How long do broccoli sprouts last in the fridge?

Properly stored in a sealed container, they last 3 to 5 days. Rinse them daily in a colander and pat dry to extend freshness.

### Do broccoli sprouts taste like broccoli?

They have a milder, slightly peppery flavor, similar to radish sprouts. They're great on sandwiches, salads, or stirred into soups at the end of cooking.

### Can I grow broccoli sprouts in soil?

Yes, you can grow them as microgreens in a tray of potting mix, but that takes 10 to 14 days and requires more space. The jar method is simpler and faster.

## The bottom line

Broccoli sprouts are the quick, cheap, nutrient-dense option; mature broccoli is the garden staple. If you have a sunny windowsill, start sprouting today. If you have a garden bed, plant broccoli transplants this weekend for a harvest in 2 to 3 months. Both are worth your time, and neither is difficult. Choose based on your space and patience, and you'll be rewarded with fresh, healthy brassicas.
