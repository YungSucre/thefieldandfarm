Designing a corn maze starts with a simple sketch and a field of tall corn. You do not need fancy software or a big crew: a paper grid, a string line, and a sharp mower will do. Here is the exact process I use on my own farm, from choosing the field to cutting the final path.

## Step 1: Choose the field and corn variety

Pick a field that is at least 1 acre for a small maze. A 1 acre maze with 3 foot wide paths gives about 1,200 linear feet of trail, enough for a 20 minute walk. For a family friendly maze, aim for 1 to 3 acres. Larger mazes (5+ acres) are for commercial attractions with multiple paths and activities.

Plant a field corn variety that grows tall and sturdy. I use 'Pioneer 1197' or 'DKC 62-08', both reach 8 to 10 feet tall. Avoid sweet corn: it is shorter and the stalks are too brittle. Plant in rows 30 inches apart, at a population of 30,000 to 34,000 seeds per acre. This gives thick stalks that hide the path walls well.

Plant in late April to early May for most of the US (USDA zones 5 to 7). The corn needs to be at least 6 feet tall before you cut the maze, usually 60 to 70 days after planting. In the South (zone 8+), plant in March or April; in the North (zone 3 to 4), wait until mid May.

## Step 2: Design the path on paper first

Do not cut anything until you have a map. Use graph paper: each square equals 10 feet of field. Draw the outer boundary, then sketch the path. Keep the path width at 3 feet for easy walking and mowing. Make the path dead end free: every turn should lead somewhere, or you will have frustrated visitors.

A simple design for beginners is a spiral or a single loop with a few dead end branches. For a more interesting maze, create a theme: a pumpkin, a star, or a farm animal. You can trace a simple shape from a coloring book or use a free online tool like Maze Generator (mazegenerator.net) to create a printable pattern.

Keep the total path length under 1 mile for a 1 acre maze. Longer paths require more cutting time and more signage. Also plan for emergency exits: cut a few straight cross paths that are wider (5 feet) and mark them with flags. These let you reach visitors quickly if needed.

## Step 3: Transfer the design to the field

Once the corn is 6 to 8 feet tall, it is time to cut. You will need:

- A string trimmer with a heavy duty blade (like a Stihl FS 90, about $300) for the initial cut.
- A lawn mower with a mulching blade (a used riding mower works, $800 to $1,500) for widening paths.
- Spray paint in a bright color (orange or pink) to mark the path edges.
- A measuring wheel (about $30) to measure distances.

Start at the field edge. Use the measuring wheel to mark the outer boundary with paint. Then, following your paper map, use the string trimmer to cut a 3 foot wide path. Walk slowly, keeping the trimmer head low to cut the stalks at ground level. Do not worry about perfection: you can widen later.

For complex designs, use a grid method. Set up stakes at 10 foot intervals along the field edges, then run strings across to create a grid. Mark each intersection on your map, then cut the path by following the grid lines. This keeps the design accurate even on a large field.

## Step 4: Cut the path and clean up

After the initial trimmer cut, go over the path with the riding mower to flatten the cut stalks and widen to 3 feet. This also packs down the soil, making walking easier. Mow at a low setting (2 inches) to keep the path clear for the season.

Remove the cut stalks from the path: they can trip visitors and hide the path edges. Rake them into the corn rows or use a leaf blower to push them aside. This takes time, but it is worth it for safety.

Finally, walk the entire maze yourself. Look for dead ends that are too long (more than 20 feet) and cut a shortcut if needed. Also check for low spots where water might pool after rain.

## Step 5: Add signs and open to visitors

Once the maze is cut, add simple signs at intersections: arrows for the correct path, or checkpoints with trivia questions. Use wooden stakes and corrugated plastic signs (about $2 each). For a themed maze, place cutouts or flags at key points.

If you charge admission, set up a simple ticket table at the entrance. Typical prices in 2026 are $8 to $12 per adult, $5 to $8 for kids under 12. A 1 acre maze can handle 50 to 100 visitors per hour, so you can gross $400 to $1,200 per day on a busy fall weekend.

## Costs and time

Here is a realistic budget for a 1 acre maze:

- Seed corn: $80 to $120 (for 1 acre)
- Fertilizer and planting: $150 to $250
- String trimmer (if you do not own one): $300
- Used riding mower: $800 to $1,500
- Signs and stakes: $50 to $100
- Total initial investment: $1,380 to $2,270 (if you need both machines)

Cutting time: a 1 acre maze with 1,200 feet of path takes 6 to 10 hours with a trimmer and mower. Plan a full day with a helper.

## FAQ

**How early can I cut the maze?**
Cut when the corn is at least 6 feet tall, usually 60 to 70 days after planting. Cutting too early stunts the stalks and the paths may close up as the corn grows.

**What if my design is too complex?**
Start simple. A spiral or a single loop is easier to cut and maintain. You can always add branches later if you want more challenge.

**How do I keep the paths from growing back?**
Corn does not regrow after you cut it, so the paths stay open. However, weeds may sprout: mow the paths every 2 weeks during the season.

**Can I use a tractor to cut the maze?**
Yes, if you have a small tractor with a finish mower, it will cut faster. But a trimmer is easier for tight turns. Many farmers use a trimmer for the first cut and a mower for cleanup.

## The bottom line

A corn maze is a doable project for any farmer with a spare acre and a weekend. Start with a simple design, cut carefully, and test the maze before opening. The key is planning on paper first: that saves you time and frustration in the field. With a $2,000 budget and 10 hours of work, you can have a profitable fall attraction that brings families to your farm.
