Agritourism farming is the practice of inviting visitors to your farm or homestead for education, recreation, or direct sales. It turns your existing operation into a destination, whether you host a pumpkin patch, a farm stay, or a workshop. This guide explains what it takes to start, what it costs, and how to do it safely and profitably.

## What counts as agritourism?

Agritourism includes any activity that brings the public to your land for agricultural purposes. Common examples are:

- U-pick orchards and berry patches
- Farm stands and roadside markets
- Corn mazes and pumpkin patches
- Farm stays and camping
- Workshops (canning, cheese making, beekeeping)
- Farm tours and school field trips
- Hayrides and seasonal festivals

You do not need to be a large farm. A half-acre homestead with a few goats and a small orchard can host a successful U-pick or a weekend workshop.

## Why start agritourism?

The main reasons are extra income and community connection. A small U-pick berry patch can earn $3,000 to $8,000 per acre per season, depending on crop and location. A farm stay can bring in $100 to $200 per night. Workshops often charge $40 to $75 per person.

Agritourism also diversifies your income, so a bad crop year does not wipe you out. And it builds local support for your farm, which helps with sales and advocacy.

## What are the costs and legal requirements?

Startup costs vary widely. A simple farm stand might cost $500 to $2,000. A U-pick operation needs signage, parking, and liability insurance, often $1,000 to $3,000 per year. A farm stay requires more: plumbing, beds, and safety upgrades, easily $10,000 to $50,000.

Legal steps are non-negotiable. You need:

- Business license and tax registration
- Liability insurance (general liability, often $500 to $2,000 per year for small operations)
- Compliance with local zoning and health codes
- Signed waivers for high-risk activities (hayrides, farm animals)
- Food safety permits if you sell prepared food or cider

Check with your county extension office and local planning department. Many states have agritourism liability laws that protect farmers, but you still need insurance and proper signage.

## How to start this week

You can take concrete steps right now, even if you are not ready to open next month.

1. **Assess your property.** Walk your land and list what you have: open fields, a barn, a pond, animals, orchard. Think about what visitors would find interesting.
2. **Choose one low-cost activity.** Start with a farm stand or a small U-pick patch. These require little infrastructure and are easy to test.
3. **Check your zoning.** Call your county planning office and ask if agritourism is allowed on your property. Some zones require a special permit.
4. **Get insurance quotes.** Call two or three local agents. Ask specifically about agritourism coverage. Get written quotes.
5. **Set a date.** Pick a weekend in the next 60 days for a soft opening. Announce it on social media and to neighbors.

## Choose crops and activities that fit your land

For U-pick, choose crops that are easy and productive. Good options for most zones:

- Strawberries (June bearing, zones 4-8): plant in early spring, harvest in June.
- Blueberries (zones 3-9): plant in fall or early spring, harvest in July-August.
- Pumpkins (zones 3-9): plant in late spring, harvest in September-October.
- Apples (zones 4-8): plant in fall, harvest in September-October.

If you prefer workshops, pick topics you know well. Canning, sourdough, and beekeeping are popular. Charge $40 to $60 per person, and cap class size at 10 to 15 for quality.

## Marketing and pricing

Set prices based on local competition. U-pick berries often run $3 to $5 per pound. Pumpkins are $5 to $15 each, depending on size. Farm stays range from $100 to $200 per night.

Market through:

- A simple website (Squarespace or Wix, $20 to $40 per month)
- Facebook and Instagram (free, but time-consuming)
- Local farmers markets and bulletin boards
- Word of mouth and email lists
- Listing on state agritourism directories

Start small. Test one activity, get feedback, then expand.

## Common mistakes to avoid

Do not skip insurance. One accident can wipe out years of profit. Do not overpromise. If you say the corn maze is open, it must be open. Do not ignore bathroom and parking needs. Visitors expect clean facilities. And do not price too low. You are offering an experience, not just a product.

## FAQ

**Do I need a lot of land to start agritourism?**
No. Many successful operations use 1 to 5 acres. A small U-pick patch or a workshop barn can fit on a half acre.

**How much can I realistically earn?**
A small farm stand might earn $1,000 to $5,000 per season. A U-pick berry patch can bring $3,000 to $8,000 per acre. A farm stay can earn $10,000 to $30,000 per year if booked regularly.

**What insurance do I need?**
At minimum, general liability insurance. If you have animals or hayrides, you may need additional coverage. Costs range from $500 to $2,000 per year for small operations.

**Can I start without a business license?**
No. You need a business license and tax registration to sell products or charge admission. Check with your local government for specifics.

## The bottom line

Agritourism is a practical way to earn extra income and connect with your community. Start small, get insured, and focus on one activity you can do well. With careful planning, you can turn your farm or homestead into a destination that pays for itself. Begin with a simple farm stand or a U-pick patch, and grow from there.
