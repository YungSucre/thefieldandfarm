If you are new to keeping chickens, the best breed for you is one that is calm, hardy, and a reliable layer. For most beginners, that means a dual-purpose breed like the Rhode Island Red or a friendly layer like the Buff Orpington. These birds are forgiving of small mistakes, tolerate a range of climates, and will give you a steady supply of eggs without demanding constant attention.

## What Makes a Breed Good for Beginners?

A good beginner breed is calm, easy to handle, and not prone to flightiness. It should also be hardy in both heat and cold, and have a low incidence of health problems. Look for breeds that are known for being docile and that do not go broody often, unless

you want to hatch chicks. Also, consider whether you want eggs, meat, or both, and how much space you have.

### Rhode Island Red

Rhode Island Reds are the classic American farm chicken. They are hardy, adaptable, and excellent layers of large brown eggs. They are active foragers, which is great if you let them free-range, but they can be a bit pushy with other chickens. They are a great choice if you want a no-nonsense bird that will produce well for years.

### Buff Orpington

If you want a chicken that will sit in your lap, the Buff Orpington is your bird. They are exceptionally calm and friendly, making them ideal for families with children. They are also cold-hardy thanks to their fluffy feathers, but they do not do as well in extreme heat. They are prone to going broody, so if you want eggs, you may need to break them of that habit.

### Australorp

Australorps are known for their gentle disposition and impressive egg production, often laying 250 to 300 eggs per year. They are also excellent foragers and do well in both confinement and free-range. They are a bit more reserved than Orpingtons but still friendly. They handle cold well and are generally healthy.

### Plymouth Rock (Barred Rock)

Plymouth Rocks are a dual-purpose breed that is both friendly and productive. They are curious and enjoy human interaction, making them a favorite for beginners. They lay brown eggs consistently and are hardy in most climates. They are also relatively quiet, which is a plus if you have close neighbors.

### White Leghorn

If your priority is maximum egg production, the White Leghorn is the champion, laying up to 320 white eggs per year. However, they are more flighty and less friendly than the others on this list. They are also excellent foragers and do well in hot climates. If you want a bird that is more of a pet, this may not be the best choice, but for eggs, it is hard to beat.

## How to Choose the Right Breed for Your Situation

Consider your climate, space, and goals. If you live in a cold northern state, choose cold-hardy breeds like Rhode Island Reds or Buff Orpingtons. If you are in the South, look for heat-tolerant breeds like Leghorns or Australorps. If you have limited space, smaller breeds like Leghorns are more active and need room to roam, so a larger breed like an Orpington might be better in a small run. Also, think about whether you want eggs, meat, or both. Dual-purpose breeds like Plymouth Rocks give you both.

## Where to Buy and What to Expect in 2026

You can buy chicks from local farm supply stores, hatcheries, or online. In 2026, expect to pay $3 to $6 per straight-run chick, $5 to $10 per sexed pullet, and $15 to $30 for a started pullet (around 16 weeks old). If you want to skip the chick stage, you can buy adult hens for $20 to $40 each, but they may not be as tame as chicks you raise yourself. Order early, especially in spring, because hatcheries sell out quickly.

## Getting Started This Week

If you are ready to start, here are steps you can take this week:

1. Check your local zoning laws to make sure you can keep chickens.
2. Decide on your breed based on your priorities (eggs, temperament, etc.).
3. Order chicks from a reputable hatchery or reserve them at a local store.
4. Set up your brooder: a large plastic tub or cardboard box with a heat lamp, pine shavings, and a chick waterer and feeder. You will need a heat lamp that keeps the brooder at 95°F for the first week, then reduce by 5°F each week.
5. Plan your coop and run: allow at least 4 square feet per bird inside the coop and 10 square feet per bird in the run. Use hardware cloth (not chicken wire) to keep out predators.

## FAQ

### How many chickens should I start with?

Start with 3 to 5 chickens. That gives you a small flock that is manageable for beginners, and they are social animals, so they need at least 2 or 3 to be happy. A flock of 3 to 5 will provide a dozen eggs a week for a family.

### Can I mix breeds?

Yes, you can mix breeds, but be aware that some breeds are more dominant than others. For example, Rhode Island Reds can be bossy, so if you mix them with docile Orpingtons, the Orpingtons may get picked on. It is often easier for beginners to start with one breed, or choose breeds with similar temperaments.

### How long do chickens live?

Most hens will lay well for 2 to 3 years, then production declines. They can live 8 to 10 years, but after 3 years, they are mostly pets. If you want a steady egg supply, plan to add new pullets every couple of years.

### Do I need a rooster for eggs?

No, hens lay eggs without a rooster. A rooster is only needed if you want fertilized eggs for hatching. If you do not want chicks, you can keep only hens, which also avoids noise and aggression issues.

## The bottom line

The best breed for you depends on your priorities, but for most beginners, the Buff Orpington or Rhode Island Red are excellent choices. They are friendly, hardy, and reliable layers. Start with 3 to 5 chicks, set up a safe coop, and you will be collecting fresh eggs in about 5 to 6 months. Do your research, order early, and enjoy the journey.
