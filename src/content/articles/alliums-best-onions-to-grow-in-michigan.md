If you're growing onions in Michigan, your best bet is to choose long-day varieties, which bulb properly when daylight reaches 14 to 16 hours. Michigan's northern location (USDA zones 4 to 6) means summer days are long enough for these types. Short-day onions, which need 10 to 12 hours of daylight, will form small bulbs or just tops in Michigan. For reliable yields, stick with long-day varieties like 'Yellow Sweet Spanish', 'Red Wing', and 'Ailsa Craig'. Plant sets or transplants in early spring, as soon as the soil can be worked, usually late March to mid-April depending on your zone.

## Understanding Day Length and Onion Types

Onions are photoperiodic: they bulb based on day length. In Michigan, summer days reach about 15 hours in June, so long-day onions are the only type that will produce full-sized bulbs. Short-day and intermediate-day onions will either not bulb at all or will produce small, poor-quality bulbs. Stick with long-day varieties for dependable results.

## Top Onion Varieties for Michigan

Here are proven long-day varieties that perform well in Michigan's climate:

| Variety | Type | Days to Maturity | Storage Potential | Notes |
|---------|------|------------------|-------------------|-------|
| 'Yellow Sweet Spanish' | Yellow | 110 days | Short to medium | Large, mild, good for fresh use; not a long keeper |
| 'Red Wing' | Red | 110 days | Medium | Deep red, stores well, disease resistant |
| 'Ailsa Craig' | Yellow | 110 days | Short | Huge, sweet onions; use fresh, not for storage |
| 'Copra' | Yellow | 105 days | Long | Excellent storage, pungent flavor |
| 'Patterson' | Yellow | 110 days | Long | Good storage, disease resistant |
| 'Red Creole' | Red | 110 days | Medium | Spicy, stores moderately; best for southern Michigan |

For storage onions, 'Copra' and 'Patterson' are top choices. For fresh eating, 'Ailsa Craig' and 'Yellow Sweet Spanish' are hard to beat. If you want red onions, 'Red Wing' is reliable and stores better than most reds.

## When to Plant in Michigan

Planting times vary by zone:

- Zone 4 (Upper Peninsula, northern Lower Peninsula): plant sets or transplants in early to mid-April.
- Zone 5 (central Lower Peninsula): plant in late March to early April.
- Zone 6 (southern Lower Peninsula, along Lake Michigan): plant in mid to late March.

If you're starting from seed, sow indoors 8 to 10 weeks before your last frost date. For most of Michigan, that means starting seeds in late February to early March. Harden off seedlings for a week before transplanting.

## Soil Preparation and Planting

Onions need well-drained soil with a pH between 6.0 and 7.0. Work in 2 to 3 inches of compost or aged manure before planting. Add a balanced fertilizer like 10-10-10 at a rate of 1 pound per 100 square feet, or use a starter solution at transplant.

Plant sets or transplants 1 inch deep, spacing them 4 to 6 inches apart in rows 12 to 18 inches apart. If you want larger onions, space them 6 inches apart; for smaller but more numerous onions, go with 4 inches.

## Caring for Onions Through the Season

- Water: Onions need about 1 inch of water per week. Keep the soil consistently moist but not waterlogged. Mulch with straw or grass clippings to retain moisture and suppress weeds.
- Fertilize: Side-dress with nitrogen (like blood meal or a high-nitrogen fertilizer) when plants are about 6 inches tall, and again when bulbs start to swell. Use about 1/2 cup per 10 feet of row.
- Weed control: Onions are poor competitors. Hand-pull weeds or use a hoe carefully. Mulch helps a lot.
- Pests: Watch for onion maggots and thrips. Use row covers early in the season to prevent maggot flies from laying eggs. For thrips, spray with insecticidal soap if you see damage.

## Harvesting and Curing

Stop watering when about 10% of the tops start to fall over. This signals the bulbs are mature. Gently lift the onions with a fork, leaving the tops attached. Cure them in a warm, dry, well-ventilated area for 2 to 3 weeks. After curing, cut the tops to 1 inch and the roots to 1/4 inch. Store in a cool, dry place (40 to 50°F) with good air circulation. 'Copra' and 'Patterson' will keep for 6 to 8 months if stored properly.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- Planting too deep: Bulbs need to sit at soil level; if planted too deep, they may rot or form elongated bulbs. Keep the top of the bulb exposed as it grows.
- Overwatering: Soggy soil leads to rot. Water deeply but less frequently.
- Choosing the wrong varieties: Short-day onions will disappoint. Always check that the variety is long-day.
- Planting too late: If you plant in May, you'll get small bulbs. Stick to the recommended dates.

## FAQ

**Can I grow onions from seed in Michigan?**
Yes, but it takes longer. Start seeds indoors 8 to 10 weeks before the last frost. You'll get a wider variety, but sets are easier for beginners.

**What's the best way to store onions?**
Cure them thoroughly, then store in a cool, dry place with good air circulation. Use mesh bags or hang them in pantyhose. Avoid storing near potatoes, which release moisture and cause sprouting.

**Why are my onions bolting?**
Bolting (flowering) is caused by temperature swings, especially cold snaps after warm spells. Plant at the right time and choose bolt-resistant varieties like 'Red Wing' or 'Patterson'.

**Can I grow onions in containers?**
Yes, but use a deep pot (at least 10 inches) and plant 3 to 4 inches apart. Choose smaller varieties like 'Red Creole' or 'Purplette'. Expect smaller yields than in-ground.

## The Bottom Line

For Michigan, choose long-day onion varieties like 'Copra', 'Patterson', 'Red Wing', or 'Ailsa Craig'. Plant sets or transplants in early spring, space them 4 to 6 inches apart, and keep the soil consistently moist. Harvest when tops fall over, cure for a few weeks, and store in a cool, dry place. With these steps, you'll have a solid onion harvest to enjoy fresh and store for winter.
