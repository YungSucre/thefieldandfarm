Onions take 90 to 120 days from seed, 60 to 80 days from sets, depending on variety and your growing zone. The exact time depends on whether you start from seed, sets, or transplants, and on day length triggers. Here's how to time it right.

## Onion types and day length

Onions bulb based on day length. Choose the right type for your latitude:

- **Short-day onions**: bulb when days reach 10 to 12 hours. Best for zones 7 and warmer (southern US). Plant in fall, harvest in late spring. Varieties: 'Red Creole', 'Texas SuperSweet', 'Yellow Granex' ( Vidalia type).
- **Long-day onions**: bulb when days reach 14 to 16 hours. Best for zones 6 and cooler (northern US). Plant in early spring, harvest in midsummer. Varieties: 'Walla Walla', 'Yellow Sweet Spanish', 'Red Wing'.
- **Day-neutral onions**: bulb regardless of day length, but do best in zones 5 and 6. Varieties: 'Candy', 'Superstar'. Plant in early spring.

If you plant the wrong type, you get small bulbs or no bulbs at all. For example, a long-day onion in Florida will not bulb because days never get long enough.

## Harvest time from seed, sets, and transplants

| Starting method | Time to harvest | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Seed (direct sow or indoor) | 90 to 120 days | Long storage, wide variety choice |
| Sets (small bulbs) | 60 to 80 days | Quick harvest, easy for beginners |
| Transplants (seedlings) | 70 to 90 days | Faster than seed, more reliable than sets |

Sets are the fastest, but they are more likely to bolt (flower early) if exposed to cold. Transplants give you a head start without the fuss of seed starting. Seeds take longest but offer the most varieties.

## Planting dates by zone (for spring harvest)

Plant onions as soon as the soil can be worked in spring, about 4 to 6 weeks before the last frost. For fall planting (short-day onions), plant 4 to 6 weeks before the first fall frost.

| USDA zone | Spring planting (long-day) | Fall planting (short-day) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 3-4 | April to early May | Not recommended |
| 5-6 | March to April | September to October |
| 7-8 | February to March | October to November |
| 9-10 | January to February | November to December |

In zone 5, I plant sets in early April and harvest in late June, about 75 days. In zone 8, you can plant short-day onions in November and harvest in May.

## How to know when onions are ready

Onions are ready when the tops flop over. Here's what to look for:

1. **Neck softens**: The stem just above the bulb feels soft, not rigid.
2. **Tops fall over**: About 50% to 80% of the tops have flopped. This is the signal to stop watering and let the soil dry.
3. **Bulb size**: The bulb is the expected size for the variety. Don't wait for maximum size; flavor and storage quality peak when tops fall.

Once tops fall, leave onions in the ground for 1 to 2 weeks if the weather is dry. This allows skins to set and improves storage. If rain is forecast, pull them immediately to avoid rot.

## Steps to harvest and cure

Harvesting is simple, but curing is critical for storage.

1. **Loosen soil**: Use a garden fork to gently lift onions, avoiding cuts. Do not pull by the tops; you may break them.
2. **Cure**: Lay onions in a single layer in a warm, dry, airy place (like a garage or porch) for 2 to 3 weeks. Keep them out of direct sun to prevent sunscald.
3. **Trim**: After curing, cut tops to 1 inch and roots to 1/4 inch. Or braid tops for hanging.
4. **Store**: Keep in a cool (32-40°F), dry place with good air circulation. Mesh bags or wire baskets work well.

Cured onions store for 2 to 6 months, depending on variety. 'Copra' and 'Redwing' store longer; 'Walla Walla' is sweet but stores only 1 to 2 months.

## Common mistakes that delay harvest

- **Planting too deep**: Bulbs need to sit on top of the soil. Plant sets so the tip is just below the surface. If buried too deep, bulbs stay small.
- **Overwatering**: Onions need consistent moisture, but soggy soil delays maturity and causes rot. Water 1 inch per week, less when tops start to fall.
- **Too much nitrogen**: High nitrogen promotes leaf growth, not bulb growth. Use a balanced fertilizer (10-10-10) at planting, then switch to a low-nitrogen feed (like 0-0-60) when bulbs start to swell.
- **Harvesting too early**: If you pull before tops fall, bulbs are immature and won't store. Wait for the natural signal.

## Tools and costs

You don't need much. A garden fork ($20 to $40) and a trowel ($10 to $15) are enough. For curing, a mesh bag or a wire rack (under $20). Seeds cost $2 to $4 per packet; sets cost $8 to $12 per pound (about 50 to 60 sets). Transplants cost $10 to $15 per bunch of 50.

## FAQ

**Can I harvest onions early as green onions?**
Yes. Pull any onion at any stage and use as scallions. This thins the row and gives you a fresh crop. Just wash and use immediately; they don't store.

**What if my onion tops don't fall over?**
If tops are still upright after the expected days, wait a week. If they still don't fall, check the bulb size. Some varieties, like 'Candy', may not flop as dramatically. You can bend the tops over manually, but this may reduce storage life.

**How do I store onions long-term?**
Cure thoroughly, then store in a cool, dry, dark place. Ideal conditions: 32-40°F, 60-70% humidity, good ventilation. Do not store in plastic bags; use mesh or paper. Check regularly and remove any sprouting or soft onions.

**Can I grow onions in containers?**
Yes. Use a pot at least 8 inches deep with drainage. Plant sets 2 inches apart. Water regularly, but don't let them sit in water. Harvest when tops fall, same as garden onions.

## The bottom line

Onions take 60 to 120 days from planting to harvest, depending on method and variety. Choose the right day-length type for your zone, plant at the right time, and watch for the tops to fall. When they do, stop watering, cure, and store. With a little patience, you'll have homegrown onions for months.
