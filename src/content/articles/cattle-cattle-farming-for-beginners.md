Starting a cattle farm is a big step, but it is manageable if you plan carefully. This guide covers the basics: choosing the right breed, fencing, feeding, health care, and realistic costs. You will know what to do this week and what to expect in the first year.

## Assess Your Land and Resources

Before buying any cattle, evaluate your land. A general rule is one cow-calf pair per 2 to 5 acres, depending on pasture quality and rainfall. In dry regions, you may need 10 or more acres per pair. Overstocking leads to overgrazing, which costs more in feed and vet bills.

Check your water supply: cattle need 20 to 30 gallons per head per day. You need a reliable source, whether a pond, stream, or well. If you do not have natural water, plan for a trough and a way to keep it from freezing in winter.

## Choose Your Breed and Purpose

Decide why you want cattle: beef, milk, or both? For beginners, beef cattle are simpler. Here are proven breeds:

- **Angus**: Hardy, good marbling, widely available. Great for beef.
- **Hereford**: Docile, efficient on grass, good for beginners.
- **Dexter**: Small size, good for small acreage, dual-purpose (milk and beef).
- **Jersey**: If you want milk, Jerseys are efficient and gentle, but they need more management.

For a homestead, a Dexter or a small Angus cross is a practical choice. They are easier to handle and require less feed than larger breeds.

## Fencing and Handling Facilities

Good fencing is non-negotiable. Cattle are strong and will test fences. Options:

- **Barbed wire**: Classic, but can injure animals. Use 4 to 5 strands, with posts every 10 to 12 feet.
- **High-tensile smooth wire**: Safer, but more expensive. Use 5 to 6 strands.
- **Electric fencing**: Effective for rotational grazing, but requires maintenance. A solar charger costs $200 to $500.

Plan for a handling system: a corral with a head gate and a squeeze chute. You will need it for vet visits, tagging, and loading. A basic setup costs $1,500 to $3,000. You can start with a simple alley and a head gate, but do not skip it.

## Feeding and Nutrition

Cattle are ruminants; they need forage. In summer, good pasture is enough. In winter, you will feed hay. Plan on 2% to 3% of body weight in dry matter per day. A 1,000-pound cow eats 20 to 30 pounds of hay daily.

Hay costs vary: $100 to $200 per ton in 2026, depending on region and quality. A cow may eat 3 to 5 tons per winter. That is $300 to $1,000 per cow.

You also need minerals. Provide a loose mineral supplement with salt, calcium, phosphorus, and trace minerals. Cost: $20 to $40 per bag (50 pounds), lasting a month or two.

If you have poor pasture, you may need supplemental grain, but that adds cost. Start with good pasture and hay.

## Health Care Basics

Cattle need routine care:

- **Vaccinations**: Core vaccines include IBR, BVD, PI3, BRSV (often combined as a 5-way). Cost: $3 to $5 per dose.
- **Deworming**: Use a dewormer in spring and fall. Cost: $5 to $10 per treatment.
- **Hoof care**: Trim hooves as needed, especially if on soft ground. A farrier can do it, but you can learn.
- **Fly control**: Use ear tags or pour-on products in summer. Cost: $10 to $20 per animal.

Find a large animal vet before you need one. Ask for a herd health plan. Budget $100 to $200 per cow per year for routine health care.

## Daily and Seasonal Chores

Cattle are not high-maintenance, but they need daily checks. In winter, you may need to break ice on water. In summer, check for signs of heat stress.

A typical day: check water, look at each animal for lameness or illness, and provide feed if needed. This takes 15 to 30 minutes.

Seasonal tasks:

- **Spring**: Calving, vaccinations, pasture rotation.
- **Summer**: Fly control, check water, mow weeds.
- **Fall**: Wean calves, deworm, prepare for winter.
- **Winter**: Feed hay, break ice, monitor body condition.

## Realistic Costs and Returns

Here is a rough budget for starting with two cows (2026 prices):

| Item | Cost Range |
| --- | --- |
| Two bred heifers or cows | $2,500 - $4,000 each |
| Fencing (5 acres) | $1,500 - $3,000 |
| Handling facility | $1,500 - $3,000 |
| Hay for first winter | $600 - $2,000 |
| Minerals and health | $200 - $400 per year |
| Misc. (tags, tools) | $100 - $300 |

Total startup: $8,000 to $15,000 for two cows. Annual costs after that: $1,000 to $2,000 per cow.

Returns: if you sell a weaned calf, expect $600 to $1,000 per calf in 2026, depending on weight and market. If you raise to slaughter weight, you might net more, but it takes longer. Many small farms break even or make a small profit, but do not expect a big income.

## First Steps This Week

1. **Call your county extension agent** to ask about local regulations and soil testing.
2. **Walk your land** and map out water sources, fencing lines, and shelter.
3. **Talk to a local cattle farmer** and ask about their costs and challenges.
4. **Set a budget** and decide on your breed and number of animals.
5. **Start building fencing** if you have a plan.

## FAQ

**Do I need a bull to start?** No. You can buy bred heifers or cows, or use artificial insemination. A bull adds cost and danger, so start with bred females.

**How much land do I really need?** For a small herd, 2 to 5 acres per cow-calf pair is a common rule, but it varies. Start with fewer animals than you think you can handle.

**Can I raise cattle on pasture alone?** Yes, if you have good grass and manage rotational grazing. But in winter, you will need hay. Some regions require supplemental feed.

**What is the easiest breed for a beginner?** Angus and Hereford are hardy and docile. Dexters are smaller and easier to handle, but may be harder to find.

## The bottom line

Cattle farming is rewarding but requires planning and investment. Start small, focus on good fencing and water, and build relationships with local vets and farmers. Expect to spend $8,000 to $15,000 to start with two cows, and plan for annual costs of $1,000 to $2,000 per cow. With careful management, you can raise healthy animals and maybe sell a calf or two. Do not rush: learn first, then buy.
