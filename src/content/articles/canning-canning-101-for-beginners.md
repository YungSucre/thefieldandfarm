Canning is a reliable way to preserve your garden harvest, and you can start this week with a water bath canner for high-acid foods like tomatoes, pickles, and jams. For low-acid vegetables and meats, you need a pressure canner. This guide walks you through the equipment, the process, and the safety rules, with real prices and steps you can follow today.

## What you need to start canning

Before your first batch, gather these tools. You likely have some already.

- **Water bath canner**: A large pot with a rack, holds 7 to 9 quart jars. Price: $30 to $50 new, or use a deep stockpot with a rack. Must be deep enough to cover jars with 1 to 2 inches of water.
- **Pressure canner**: For low-acid foods. A 23-quart model with a dial or weighted gauge costs $100 to $150. This is a one-time investment that lasts decades.
- **Glass jars**: Mason jars in sizes from half-pint to quart. A case of 12 quart jars runs $15 to $20. Reuse jars from store-bought foods only if they are Mason brand; other jars may crack.
- **Lids and bands**: New lids each use, about $3 to $4 per dozen. Bands are reusable.
- **Canning tools**: Jar lifter, funnel, bubble remover, and magnetic lid lifter. A starter kit costs $15 to $25.
- **Optional but helpful**: Kitchen scale, timer, and a notebook for tracking batches.

## Water bath canning step by step

Water bath canning is for high-acid foods: fruits, pickles, jams, and tomatoes with added acid. The boiling water kills bacteria and creates a vacuum seal.

1. **Prepare your jars**: Wash jars in hot soapy water, rinse, and keep them hot in the canner or oven (200°F) until filling.
2. **Prepare your recipe**: Use a tested recipe from a reliable source like the USDA or Ball Blue Book. Do not alter vinegar, sugar, or acid proportions.
3. **Fill jars**: Leave headspace as specified, usually 1/4 inch for jams, 1/2 inch for pickles. Remove air bubbles with a bubble remover.
4. **Wipe rims**: Use a clean, damp cloth to remove any food residue. This is critical for sealing.
5. **Apply lids**: Center the lid on the jar, screw the band on fingertip tight (not too tight).
6. **Process**: Place jars in the canner with boiling water, cover, and bring back to a rolling boil. Start timing once boiling. Process for the time in your recipe.
7. **Cool and check seals**: Remove jars and cool on a towel for 12 to 24 hours. Press the center of each lid; it should not flex. Refrigerate any unsealed jars and use within a week.

For example, a basic dill pickle recipe with cucumbers, vinegar, and salt processes for 10 minutes in a boiling water bath at altitudes below 1,000 feet. Adjust time for higher altitudes: add 1 minute per 1,000 feet above sea level.

## Pressure canning basics

For low-acid foods like green beans, carrots, corn, and meats, you must use a pressure canner. The high temperature (240°F) kills botulism spores that boiling water cannot.

- **Add water**: Put 2 to 3 inches of hot water in the canner.
- **Load jars**: Place filled jars on the rack, leaving space between them.
- **Lock the lid**: Follow your canner's manual. Heat until steam escapes steadily, then vent for 10 minutes.
- **Pressurize**: Close the vent or place the weighted gauge. Bring to the required pressure: 10 to 11 psi for most foods at sea level.
- **Process**: Start timing once pressure is reached. Keep pressure steady. For example, pint jars of green beans process for 20 minutes at 10 psi.
- **Cool naturally**: Turn off heat and let pressure drop to zero. Do not force-cool. Wait 2 to 3 minutes, then open the lid away from your face.

Always check your altitude and adjust pressure: add 1 psi for every 2,000 feet above 1,000 feet for dial gauges, or use the next weight setting for weighted gauges.

## Safety rules you cannot skip

- **Use tested recipes only**: No old family recipes unless they match current USDA guidelines. Acid levels change with variety and ripeness.
- **Check jar seals**: Unsealed jars go in the fridge.
- **Boil before tasting**: If you have any doubt, boil low-acid canned food for 10 minutes before eating.
- **Store properly**: Remove bands, wipe jars, and store in a cool, dark place. Use within 12 months for best quality.
- **Discard if spoiled**: Bulging lids, leaks, off-odors, or mold mean toss it. Do not taste.

## What to can first: easy wins for beginners

Start with high-acid foods that are forgiving and quick.

| Food | Type | Processing time (pints) | Difficulty |
|------|------|-------------------------|------------|
| Strawberry jam | Water bath | 10 minutes | Easy |
| Dill pickles | Water bath | 10 minutes | Easy |
| Tomato sauce | Water bath (add lemon juice) | 35 minutes | Moderate |
| Green beans | Pressure canner | 20 minutes at 10 psi | Moderate |

For your first batch, try a simple jam. Use 4 cups crushed strawberries, 2 cups sugar, and 1 packet of pectin. You will get about 4 half-pint jars. It takes 30 minutes of prep and 10 minutes of processing.

## FAQ

**Can I reuse store-bought jars like spaghetti sauce jars?**  
Only if they are Mason jars. Other jars may not withstand heat and can crack. Check the bottom for the Mason logo.

**How long do canned goods last?**  
Best quality within 12 months, but they are safe indefinitely if seals remain intact. After a year, texture and color may decline.

**Do I need a pressure canner for tomatoes?**  
No, if you add acid. Add 1 tablespoon of bottled lemon juice or 1/4 teaspoon citric acid per pint, then water bath process. This is safe and tested.

**What if my jars don't seal?**  
Refrigerate and use within a week, or reprocess within 24 hours with new lids. Check for nicks on the jar rim and clean thoroughly.

## The bottom line

Canning is a practical skill that saves money and gives you shelf-stable food from your garden. Start with a water bath canner and a simple jam or pickle recipe. Follow tested recipes and safety rules exactly. Once you master the basics, expand to pressure canning for low-acid foods. Your first batch will take a few hours, but the payoff is a pantry full of your own harvest.
