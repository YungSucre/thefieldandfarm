The best fence for cattle is a high-tensile electric fence: it is cost-effective, easy to install, and safe for your herd. For permanent perimeter fencing, use 5 to 6 strands of high-tensile wire with a powerful energizer. For interior divisions, a single or double hot wire works well. This guide breaks down the options, real costs, and step-by-step installation so you can make the right choice for your farm.

## What you need for a high-tensile electric fence

1. **Energizer**: Choose a low-impedance energizer rated for your fence length. For a 1-mile fence, you need at least 1 joule of output. Prices range from $150 for a small unit to $500 for a heavy-duty model. Brands like Gallagher, Parmak, and Speedrite are reliable.
2. **Wire**: 12.5-gauge high-tensile wire is standard. A 4,000-foot roll costs about $100 to $150. You will need 5 to 6 strands for cattle.
3. **Posts**: Use wood or steel line posts every 30 to 50 feet, and corner posts with bracing at every turn. Wood posts cost $5 to $10 each; steel T-posts cost $3 to $5. Corner assemblies need extra bracing.
4. **Insulators**: Porcelain or plastic insulators for wood posts, and clip-on insulators for T-posts. Budget $20 to $50 for a mile.
5. **Grounding system**: At least 3 ground rods, 8 feet long, spaced 10 feet apart, connected to the energizer. This is critical: poor grounding is the #1 cause of weak shocks.
6. **Tools**: Post driver, wire strainer, crimping tool, and a voltmeter. Total cost if you do not own them: $150 to $300.

## Step-by-step installation

1. **Plan your layout**: Mark corners and gate locations. Use a GPS or measuring wheel to get exact distances.
2. **Set corner posts**: Use 6-8 inch diameter wood posts, set 3 feet deep in concrete. Brace corners with diagonal posts.
3. **Run line posts**: Space them 30 to 50 feet apart. T-posts are fine for most soils; wood posts are better for rocky ground.
4. **Install insulators**: Attach insulators to each post at the same height. For cattle, place wires at 12, 24, 36, 48, and 60 inches from the ground, plus a bottom wire at 6 inches if needed.
5. **String the wire**: Unroll wire along the line, then use a strainer to tension each strand. Leave a little slack for temperature changes.
6. **Connect the energizer**: Attach the fence wire to the energizer's output terminal, and connect the ground rods to the ground terminal. Use a voltmeter to check voltage at the far end: it should be at least 3,000 volts.
7. **Test and adjust**: Walk the fence and check for shorts (vegetation touching wires). Clear any brush. If voltage is low, add more ground rods or upgrade the energizer.

## Maintenance and troubleshooting

Check your fence monthly: look for broken wires, loose insulators, and vegetation growth. Keep the fence line clear with a string trimmer or herbicide. In winter, snow and ice can cause shorts; use a high-quality energizer with a built-in lightning protector. If cattle are escaping, the fence is not delivering enough shock: test voltage and improve grounding.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many strands of wire do I need for cattle?**
For cattle, use 5 to 6 strands of high-tensile wire. The bottom wire should be 6 to 12 inches off the ground to discourage calves from crawling under, and the top wire at 54 to 60 inches for mature cows.

**Can I use a solar-powered energizer?**
Yes, solar energizers work well for remote areas. They cost $200 to $500 and include a battery and solar panel. Make sure the panel gets full sun and the battery is large enough for your fence length.

**How do I keep the fence from shorting out?**
Keep vegetation away from the wires. Use a weed trimmer or apply a non-selective herbicide like glyphosate along the fence line. Check after storms for fallen branches.

**Is electric fencing safe for calves?**
Yes, electric fencing is safe for calves. The shock is a deterrent, not harmful. Introduce calves to the fence early so they learn to respect it.

## The bottom line

For cattle, a high-tensile electric fence is the best combination of cost, durability, and safety. Plan your layout, invest in a quality energizer and grounding, and install it right the first time. Expect to spend $1,400 to $2,500 per mile if you do it yourself. With regular maintenance, your fence will last decades and keep your herd where they belong.
