Onions take 90 to 120 days from seed to harvest, depending on the variety and whether you start from seed, sets, or transplants. For most gardeners, that means planting in early spring and harvesting in midsummer, but the exact timeline depends on your USDA zone and day length. Here's the breakdown.

## Onion Growth Timeline by Method

How you start onions changes the total time from planting to harvest:

- **From seed**: 100 to 120 days. Sow seeds indoors 8 to 10 weeks before your last frost date, or direct sow 4 weeks before last frost. This gives you the widest variety choice but takes the longest.
- **From sets (small bulbs)**: 80 to 100 days. Plant sets 2 to 4 weeks before your last frost. Sets are the easiest for beginners and give a head start.
- **From transplants (started plants)**: 70 to 90 days. Buy transplants or grow your own from seed indoors. Transplants are the fastest route to harvest.

In real terms, if you plant sets in early April in Zone 6, you'll harvest in late July. If you start seeds in February, you'll harvest around the same time, but you'll be tending seedlings for two months first.

## Day Length Matters: Short Day, Long Day, Intermediate

Onions bulb based on day length, not just calendar days. Choose varieties that match your latitude:

- **Short-day onions** (for Zones 7 and warmer): Start bulbing when days reach 10 to 12 hours. Plant in fall or late winter for summer harvest. Varieties: 'Texas SuperSweet', 'Red Creole', 'Yellow Granex'.
- **Long-day onions** (for Zones 6 and cooler): Need 14 to 16 hours of daylight. Plant in early spring. Varieties: 'Walla Walla', 'Yellow Sweet Spanish', 'Red Wing'.
- **Intermediate-day onions** (Zones 5 to 7): Bulb at 12 to 14 hours. Plant in early spring. Varieties: 'Candy', 'Super Star', 'Red Candy Apple'.

If you plant the wrong type for your zone, you'll get lots of green tops but small bulbs. Check your zone and choose accordingly.

## Step-by-Step: From Planting to Harvest

Here's what to do, week by week, for a typical spring planting in Zones 3 to 6:

1. **8 to 10 weeks before last frost**: Start seeds indoors. Use a seed-starting mix, plant seeds 1/4 inch deep, and keep soil at 65 to 75°F. Provide 12 to 14 hours of light per day.
2. **2 to 4 weeks before last frost**: Harden off seedlings and plant them outdoors. Space transplants 4 to 6 inches apart in rows 12 to 18 inches apart. For sets, plant 1 inch deep, with the tip just below the surface.
3. **Spring through early summer**: Keep soil consistently moist, about 1 inch of water per week. Fertilize with a balanced fertilizer (10-10-10) at planting and again when tops are 6 inches tall. Stop fertilizing when bulbs start to swell.
4. **When tops flop over**: About 2 to 3 weeks before harvest, the tops will yellow and fall over. This signals the bulbs are mature. Gently push tops down to speed curing, but don't cut them.
5. **Harvest**: Pull onions when tops are mostly brown and dry. This usually happens 90 to 120 days after planting, depending on variety. In Zone 6, that's typically late July to early August.

## Realistic Varieties and Tools

Choose varieties proven for your zone. Here are reliable options:

- **Long-day**: 'Walla Walla' (mild, sweet, 100 days), 'Yellow Sweet Spanish' (storage, 110 days), 'Red Wing' (red, 105 days).
- **Intermediate**: 'Candy' (sweet, 100 days), 'Super Star' (white, 100 days).
- **Short-day**: 'Texas SuperSweet' (mild, 100 days), 'Red Creole' (spicy, 95 days).

Tools you'll need: a garden trowel ($10 to $20), a soil thermometer ($15 to $25), and a watering can or drip hose. For seed starting, a heat mat ($20 to $30) and grow light ($30 to $50) are worth it if you start from seed.

## Common Mistakes That Delay Harvest

Avoid these to keep your onions on schedule:

- **Planting too deep**: Sets planted deeper than 1 inch will form elongated bulbs and take longer to mature.
- **Overcrowding**: Onions need 4 to 6 inches of space. Crowded onions stay small and never bulb properly.
- **Inconsistent watering**: Onions need steady moisture. Drought stress can cause premature bulbing or stunted growth.
- **Wrong day length**: As mentioned, planting a short-day variety in the North will give you tops only. Check your zone first.

## Curing and Storing: Extending Your Harvest

After pulling, cure onions for 2 to 3 weeks in a warm, dry, well-ventilated spot. Lay them on a screen or hang them in bundles. Once the necks are dry and papery, trim the roots and tops, and store in a cool, dry place (40 to 50°F). Properly cured onions can last 3 to 6 months, depending on variety. Sweet onions like 'Walla Walla' store for only a month or two, so eat them first.

## FAQ

**Can I grow onions in containers?**
Yes, but use a container at least 12 inches deep and 12 inches wide. Plant sets 4 inches apart. You'll get smaller bulbs, but it works for patio gardeners.

**What happens if I plant onions in the fall?**
In Zones 7 and warmer, you can plant short-day varieties in fall for a spring harvest. In colder zones, fall planting often fails unless you use hardy multiplier onions like 'Egyptian Walking'.

**How do I know when onions are ready to harvest?**
When the tops flop over and start to yellow, that's the signal. Check a bulb: if it's the size you expect and the skin is papery, pull it. Don't wait too long or they may split.

**Can I speed up growth with fertilizer?**
High-nitrogen fertilizer early on helps tops grow, but too much late in the season delays bulbing. Switch to a low-nitrogen fertilizer (like 5-10-10) when bulbs start to swell.

## The Bottom Line

Onions take 90 to 120 days from seed, 80 to 100 days from sets, and 70 to 90 days from transplants. Choose the right day-length type for your zone, plant at the right time, and keep moisture steady. Start with sets for the easiest success, and you'll be pulling bulbs by midsummer. Plan your planting date backward from your expected harvest: count back 100 days from your desired harvest date, and that's your planting window. For most of the US, that means planting in early spring and harvesting in July or August.
