If you're in Ohio, you're in luck: onions grow well here, but choosing the right type and variety is key. For the best results, grow short-day or intermediate-day onions, not long-day types, because Ohio's daylight hours in summer are not long enough for long-day onions to bulb properly. This guide covers the top varieties, planting times, and steps to get a great harvest.

## Understanding Onion Day Lengths

Onions bulb based on day length. In Ohio (USDA zones 5 and 6), the summer days are about 14 to 15 hours long. Long-day onions need 14-16 hours of daylight, which is marginal here, so they often produce small bulbs or don't bulb at all. Short-day onions need 10-12 hours, so they bulb too early in Ohio, resulting in small, poor-quality onions. The sweet spot is intermediate-day onions, which need 12-14 hours. These are sometimes called "day-neutral" or "middle-day" onions.

## When and How to Plant

### Starting from Sets vs. Seeds vs. Transplants

You can grow onions from seeds, sets (small bulbs), or transplants. For Ohio, transplants give the best results because they get a head start on the growing season. Sets are easier but often bolt (go to seed) if they are large. Seeds are cheapest but need a long indoor start.

- **Transplants**: Buy from a local nursery or online. Plant in early April, as soon as the soil can be worked. Space them 4-6 inches apart in rows 12-18 inches apart. Plant the roots down, about 1 inch deep.
- **Sets**: Plant small sets (less than 3/4 inch diameter) in mid-April. Space 2-4 inches apart. Larger sets are more likely to bolt.
- **Seeds**: Start indoors 8-10 weeks before the last frost (around mid-February in Ohio). Sow seeds 1/4 inch deep in trays. Harden off and transplant in April.

### Soil Preparation

Onions need well-drained, loose soil with a pH between 6.0 and 7.0. Work in 2-3 inches of compost before planting. Avoid fresh manure, which can cause rot. If your soil is heavy clay, consider raised beds.

### Fertilizing

Onions are heavy feeders. At planting, mix in a balanced fertilizer like 10-10-10 at a rate of 1 pound per 50 square feet. Side-dress with nitrogen (like blood meal) three weeks after planting, and again when bulbs start to form. Stop fertilizing once the bulbs push the soil aside.

## Care During the Growing Season

- **Watering**: Onions need about 1 inch of water per week. Keep the soil consistently moist but not soggy. Mulch with straw to retain moisture and suppress weeds.
- **Weeding**: Onions have shallow roots, so hand-pull weeds. Avoid hoeing too deep.
- **Pests and Diseases**: Watch for onion maggots and thrips. Use row covers to prevent maggots. For thrips, spray with insecticidal soap if you see damage. Rotate crops to avoid disease.

## Harvesting and Curing

Onions are ready to harvest when the tops flop over and start to yellow. This is usually in late July to early August for intermediate-day onions. Stop watering about two weeks before harvest.

To harvest, gently loosen the soil with a fork and pull the onions. Leave them in the field for a day to dry, then move them to a well-ventilated, shaded area to cure. Cure for 2-3 weeks until the necks are dry and the skins are papery. Then trim the roots and tops, leaving 1 inch of top.

## Storing Onions

Store cured onions in a cool, dry, dark place with good air circulation. Ideal temperature is 32-40°F with 60-70% humidity. Sweet onions like 'Candy' don't store long, so eat them within 1-2 months. Storage varieties like 'Talon' can last 6-8 months if kept properly.

## Common Problems and Solutions

- **Bolting**: Caused by cold snaps or large sets. Plant sets that are small, and cover young plants if a late frost is predicted.
- **Small Bulbs**: Often due to planting too late or too close. Plant early and thin to proper spacing.
- **Rotting**: Usually from poor drainage or overwatering. Improve soil drainage and avoid overhead watering.

## FAQ

**Can I grow long-day onions in Ohio?**

Technically, you can, but they will likely produce small bulbs because Ohio's daylight hours are at the low end of what long-day onions need. Intermediate-day varieties are more reliable.

**When should I plant onion sets in Ohio?**

Plant sets in mid-April, as soon as the soil is workable. If you plant too early and a hard freeze hits, they may bolt.

**How many onions can I expect from a 10-foot row?**

With 4-inch spacing, a 10-foot row gives you about 30 onions. If you space them 6 inches apart, you'll get about 20, but they'll be larger.

**What's the best way to store sweet onions?**

Sweet onions have higher water content, so they don't store as long. Use them within a month or two. You can also chop and freeze them for longer storage.

## The bottom line

For Ohio, stick with intermediate-day onion varieties like 'Candy', 'Red Candy Apple', and 'Talon'. Plant transplants or small sets in early April, give them loose, fertile soil, and keep them watered. Harvest when tops flop, cure properly, and you'll have a good supply of onions for months. Avoid long-day types, and don't plant too late. With these steps, you'll be pulling sweet, flavorful onions by midsummer.
