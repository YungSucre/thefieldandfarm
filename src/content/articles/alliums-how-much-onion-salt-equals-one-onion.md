If you're out of fresh onions, you can swap in onion salt, but the conversion isn't one-to-one. One medium onion (about 1 cup chopped) equals roughly 1 tablespoon of onion salt, but you must reduce the added salt in your recipe to avoid over-salting. This guide gives you the exact numbers, the math, and the practical steps to make the substitution work in any dish.

## The basic conversion

A medium onion weighs about 5 to 6 ounces and yields about 1 cup chopped or 1/2 cup minced. When dehydrated and ground, that same onion produces about 1 to 2 tablespoons of onion powder. Onion salt is simply onion powder mixed with salt, typically in a 1:1 ratio by volume, though some brands use more salt. So, to replace one medium onion:

- Use 1 tablespoon of onion salt.
- Reduce the recipe's added salt by 1/2 to 1 teaspoon (since that tablespoon of onion salt contains roughly 1/2 to 1 teaspoon of salt).

## Why the math works

Onion powder is concentrated: it's the dehydrated essence of the onion, with most of the water removed. One tablespoon of onion powder equals about 1/4 cup of fresh chopped onion in flavor intensity. Since onion salt is half powder, half salt, you need double the powder volume to get the same onion flavor, hence 1 tablespoon of onion salt for a medium onion. The salt content is the catch: you're adding salt along with the onion flavor, so you must adjust the recipe's salt.

## How to substitute without ruining your dish

1. **Start with less**: Use 3/4 of the calculated onion salt, taste, and add more if needed. This prevents over-salting.
2. **Reduce other salt**: Cut the recipe's salt by 1 teaspoon for every tablespoon of onion salt you use. If the recipe has no added salt (like a salad dressing), be cautious: the onion salt alone may be enough.
3. **Rehydrate for texture**: If you need the texture of fresh onion (in salsas or salads), rehydrate the onion salt in a little water (1 tablespoon onion salt to 2 tablespoons water, let sit 5 minutes) before adding. This won't give you the crunch, but it softens the granules.
4. **Use in cooked dishes**: Onion salt works best in soups, stews, casseroles, and sauces where moisture is present. For dry rubs or coatings, it's fine as is.

## Onion salt vs. onion powder: know the difference

Onion powder is pure ground dehydrated onion, no salt. Onion salt is a blend, usually 1 part powder to 1 part salt by volume, but check the label: some brands use 2 parts salt to 1 part powder. If you have onion powder, you can make your own onion salt: mix 1 part onion powder with 1 part fine salt (by volume). For one medium onion, use 2 teaspoons of onion powder plus 1 teaspoon of salt (or to taste).

## Real-world examples

- **Soups and stews**: For a pot of beef stew serving 6, if the recipe calls for 1 large onion, use 1 1/2 tablespoons of onion salt, and reduce the added salt by 1 teaspoon. Taste after 10 minutes of simmering.
- **Salad dressings**: For a vinaigrette that calls for 1/4 cup minced shallot (about 1 small onion), use 1 1/2 teaspoons of onion salt, and omit any salt in the recipe.
- **Dry rubs**: For a BBQ rub that calls for 2 tablespoons onion powder, use 4 tablespoons (1/4 cup) of onion salt, but cut the salt in the rub by 2 teaspoons. This is a rare case where you might want to use onion powder instead to control salt.

## How to make your own onion salt

If you grow onions, you can make onion salt at home. Slice 2 pounds of onions (like 'Yellow Granex' or 'Red Burgermaster') thinly, dehydrate at 135°F for 8 to 10 hours until brittle, then grind in a spice grinder. Mix 1 part powder with 1 part fine sea salt. Store in an airtight jar; it keeps for 6 months. A 2-pound batch yields about 1/2 cup of onion powder, which makes 1 cup of onion salt. Cost: about $3 for the onions, versus $5 to $7 for a 4-ounce jar of commercial onion salt.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Using onion salt in place of fresh in salads**: The texture won't match. Rehydrate or use a different substitute.
- **Forgetting to reduce salt**: This is the #1 mistake. Always cut the recipe's salt.
- **Using garlic salt interchangeably**: They are not the same; garlic salt has a different flavor profile and salt ratio.
- **Over-measuring**: Onion salt is fine, so a level tablespoon is correct. Don't heap.

## FAQ

**Can I use onion salt in place of onion powder?**
Yes, but adjust: use 2 times the amount of onion salt as onion powder, and reduce salt elsewhere. For example, if a recipe calls for 1 teaspoon onion powder, use 2 teaspoons onion salt and cut salt by 1/4 teaspoon.

**What if I only have onion flakes?**
Onion flakes are larger and less concentrated. Use 1/2 cup of flakes for one medium onion, and rehydrate in water for 10 minutes before using. They contain no salt, so adjust salt normally.

**Does onion salt go bad?**
It lasts 2 to 3 years if stored in a cool, dark place. It won't spoil, but it loses potency. Test by smelling: if it's weak, use more.

**How much onion salt equals one small onion?**
About 1 1/2 teaspoons. A small onion yields about 1/2 cup chopped.

## The bottom line

One medium onion equals 1 tablespoon of onion salt, but always reduce the recipe's salt by 1/2 to 1 teaspoon to compensate. For the best flavor, use onion salt in cooked dishes, not raw preparations. If you're canning or preserving, stick with fresh onions for texture and safety. Keep a jar of onion salt on hand for quick meals, but don't expect it to replace the sweetness and crunch of a fresh onion. Test with a small batch first, and adjust to your taste.
