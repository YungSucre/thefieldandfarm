## What to look for in a beginner pressure canner

Before you buy, understand the two main types: weighted-gauge and dial-gauge. Weighted-gauge canners (like Presto) regulate pressure automatically with a rocker that jiggles, so you don't have to watch a dial. Dial-gauge canners (like All American) require you to adjust heat to keep the needle at the right pressure, which is more precise but more hands-on. For a beginner, a weighted-gauge is simpler and less prone to error.

Key features to consider:

- **Size**: 16 to 23 quarts is ideal. A 23-quart canner holds 7 quart jars or 9 pint jars, enough for a small harvest. Smaller canners (like 10-quart) are too small for quart jars and limit your batch size.
- **Material**: Aluminum is lighter and heats quickly; stainless steel is more durable but heavier and pricier. Presto uses aluminum, All American uses cast aluminum (very heavy).
- **Gauge**: Weighted gauges are foolproof; dial gauges need annual testing. Some models have both, like the Presto 23-quart with a dial and a weighted gauge option.
- **Safety features**: Look for an overpressure plug and a locking lid that prevents opening while pressurized. All USDA-approved canners have these.

## How to choose the right size

For a beginner, a 23-quart canner is the sweet spot. It's large enough to process a full load of quarts, which is the most common size for vegetables, meats, and soups. If you only can pints (like jams or sauces), a 16-quart model works, but you'll soon outgrow it. Consider your stove: if you have a glass-top, check the manual for weight limits and use a canner that's not too heavy when full. A full 23-quart canner can weigh 40 to 50 pounds, so make sure your stove can handle it.

## Step-by-step: Your first pressure canning session

1. **Read the manual**: Every canner is different. Familiarize yourself with the parts, especially the vent pipe and safety plug.
2. **Check your jars**: Use only Mason jars (Ball, Kerr) with new lids. Inspect for cracks or chips.
3. **Prepare your recipe**: Use a tested recipe from the USDA or a trusted source like the National Center for Home Food Preservation. Don't improvise.
4. **Add water**: Put 2 to 3 inches of hot water in the canner. Place the filled jars on the rack, ensuring they don't touch the bottom.
5. **Lock the lid and heat**: Turn the heat to high. When steam comes out the vent, let it vent for 10 minutes to exhaust air. Then place the weighted gauge on (or close the petcock for dial gauges).
6. **Bring to pressure**: Watch for the gauge to reach 10 or 11 psi (adjust for altitude). Start your timer once you reach pressure.
7. **Maintain pressure**: Adjust heat to keep the gauge steady. For weighted gauges, you should hear 2 to 3 jiggles per minute.
8. **Cool and open**: After processing time, turn off heat and let the canner cool naturally. Do not force-cool. When the pressure gauge reads zero, wait 10 more minutes, then open the lid away from your face.
9. **Check seals**: After 12 to 24 hours, press the center of each lid. It should not pop. Store jars without rings.

## Safety tips for beginners

- Always vent steam for 10 minutes before pressurizing. This prevents false pressure readings.
- Never open the canner until pressure is fully released. Forcing it can cause burns or jar breakage.
- Check your dial gauge annually: take it to a Cooperative Extension office or test it yourself with a known-good gauge.
- Replace the rubber gasket (on Presto/Mirro) every 2 to 3 years, or if it's cracked or stretched.
- Use only tested recipes. Botulism is a real risk with low-acid foods, so follow processing times and pressures exactly.

## FAQ

**Q: Can I use a pressure canner on a glass-top stove?**
A: Yes, but check your stove's manual for weight limits. Use a canner that's not too heavy when full, and avoid sliding it. Presto and Mirro are safe on glass-top if you use medium heat and don't jiggle the pot.

**Q: How often do I need to replace the gauge or gasket?**
A: For Presto and Mirro, replace the rubber gasket every 2 to 3 years or if it's damaged. Dial gauges should be tested annually; weighted gauges rarely need replacement.

**Q: What's the difference between a pressure cooker and a pressure canner?**
A: A pressure canner is larger and designed to hold jars at a specific pressure for long times. A pressure cooker is smaller and not suitable for canning because it can't maintain consistent pressure for the required duration. Use only a canner for canning.

**Q: Do I need to buy a new canner every few years?**
A: No, a good canner lasts decades. Replace gaskets and gauges as needed. All American canners are built to last a lifetime.
