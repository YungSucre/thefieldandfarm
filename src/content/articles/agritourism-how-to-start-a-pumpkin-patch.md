Starting a pumpkin patch is a rewarding project, but it takes planning and space. You need at least a quarter acre for a viable patch, full sun, and well-drained soil. This guide covers the essentials: choosing varieties, preparing soil, planting, caring for vines, and harvesting. Expect to invest $200 to $500 in seeds and supplies for a quarter acre, and you can harvest 10,000 to 20,000 pounds of pumpkins if conditions cooperate.

## Choose the right varieties

Pick varieties based on your market: decorative, carving, or pie pumpkins. For agritourism, you want a mix of sizes and colors. Here are proven varieties for 2026:

- **Howden**: Classic carving pumpkin, 18 to 25 pounds, 95 to 110 days. Reliable and disease resistant.
- **Sugar Pie**: Small, sweet, 6 to 8 pounds, 95 days. Ideal for pies and baking.
- **Jarrahdale**: Blue-gray, 8 to 12 pounds, 100 days. Unusual color attracts customers.
- **Cinderella**: Deep ribbed, 15 to 20 pounds, 100 days. Great for decoration and fairy tale themes.
- **Baby Boo**: Mini white, 1 to 2 pounds, 85 days. Popular for kids and table displays.

For a patch, plant at least three varieties to spread harvest and appeal. Buy seeds from reputable suppliers like Johnny's Selected Seeds or Burpee. A quarter pound of seed (about 500 seeds) costs $40 to $60 and covers a quarter acre.

## Prepare the soil and site

Pumpkins need rich, well-drained soil with a pH between 6.0 and 6.8. Test soil in fall or early spring. Add compost or aged manure at 2 to 3 cubic yards per 1,000 square feet. Work it into the top 8 inches. Avoid heavy clay or waterlogged areas.

Choose a site with at least 8 hours of direct sun. If you have limited space, consider using a trellis for small varieties, but for a traditional patch, plan on sprawling vines. Clear all weeds and grass before planting. Use a rototiller or hand tools to break up soil.

## Planting dates and spacing

Pumpkins are warm-season crops. Plant after the last frost when soil temperature is at least 60°F. For most zones:

- **Zone 3-4**: Late May to early June.
- **Zone 5-6**: Mid-May to late May.
- **Zone 7-8**: Early April to mid-May.
- **Zone 9-10**: February to March for fall harvest.

Sow seeds directly 1 inch deep. For hills, plant 4 to 6 seeds per hill, then thin to 2 to 3 plants. Space hills 6 to 8 feet apart in rows 10 to 12 feet apart. For vining varieties, give 5 to 10 feet between plants. If you start indoors, use biodegradable pots and transplant after frost, but direct sowing is easier and cheaper.

## Care and maintenance

Keep the soil consistently moist, especially during flowering and fruit set. Water at the base to avoid wet leaves, which invite powdery mildew. Use drip irrigation or soaker hoses for efficiency. Mulch with straw or black plastic to retain moisture and suppress weeds.

Fertilize at planting with a balanced 10-10-10 at 1 pound per 100 square feet. Side-dress with nitrogen when vines start to run, then switch to phosphorus and potassium when flowers appear. Avoid excess nitrogen, which leads to lush vines but few fruits.

Pollination is critical. Bees and other insects do the work. If you see few bees, hand-pollinate by transferring pollen from male flowers to female flowers with a small brush. Female flowers have a small swelling at the base.

## Pest and disease management

Common pests include squash bugs, cucumber beetles, and vine borers. Check undersides of leaves weekly. Remove eggs and beetles by hand. Use row covers until flowering, then remove to allow pollination. For vine borers, inject Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) into the stem or use a preventative spray.

Powdery mildew is the main disease. Space plants well, water at the base, and apply a sulfur-based fungicide if needed. Rotate crops every 3 years to reduce disease pressure.

## Harvest and storage

Pumpkins are ready when the rind is hard and the stem is dry and corky. Cut the stem with a sharp knife, leaving 3 to 4 inches. Do not carry by the stem. Cure in the sun for 10 to 14 days to harden the skin. Store in a cool, dry place at 50 to 60°F. Properly cured pumpkins last 2 to 3 months.

Yield varies by variety and care. A quarter acre can produce 10,000 to 20,000 pounds. At a retail price of $0.50 to $1.00 per pound, gross revenue can be $5,000 to $20,000, but costs include seeds, fertilizer, water, and labor. For agritourism, charge per pumpkin or by weight, and add value with hayrides and corn mazes.

## Marketing and sales

If you sell at a farm stand or farmers market, price by size: small pumpkins $3 to $5, medium $6 to $10, large $12 to $20. For u-pick, charge $0.50 to $0.75 per pound. Advertise on social media and local bulletin boards. Offer school groups a field trip experience.

## FAQ

**How much land do I need for a pumpkin patch?**
A quarter acre is a good start. It yields enough for a small farm stand or u-pick operation. For a family, a 10x10 foot patch can grow 5 to 10 pumpkins.

**Can I grow pumpkins in containers?**
Yes, but only small varieties like Baby Boo or Sugar Pie. Use a 20-gallon container or larger, and provide a trellis. Expect 2 to 4 pumpkins per plant.

**When should I plant pumpkins for Halloween?**
Count back from your expected harvest date. Most varieties need 90 to 110 days. For a Halloween harvest, plant in late May to mid-June in most zones. Check your local frost dates.

**How do I know when a pumpkin is ripe?**
The rind is hard, the stem is dry and brown, and the pumpkin sounds hollow when tapped. The color should be fully developed, but some varieties change color early.

## The bottom line

Starting a pumpkin patch is doable with careful planning. Choose the right varieties, prepare soil well, plant at the right time, and manage pests. Expect a learning curve, but with attention to detail, you can harvest a satisfying crop. Start small, keep notes, and expand as you gain experience. Your first patch may not be perfect, but it will teach you what works for your land and market.
