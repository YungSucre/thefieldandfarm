The best time to bring outdoor plants inside is about two weeks before your average first frost date, but the exact timing depends on your plant type and USDA zone. For tender tropicals like hibiscus or mandevilla, move them when nighttime temperatures consistently drop below 50°F (10°C), which is usually 2 to 4 weeks before frost. For hardy perennials in pots, you can wait until frost threatens, but for most houseplants that summered outside, the safe window is when nights stay above 55°F (13°C) to avoid cold shock.

## Know your frost date

Your first frost date is the key. Check the USDA Plant Hardiness Zone map or your local extension service for the average date. For example:

- Zone 5: first frost around October 15
- Zone 7: around November 15
- Zone 9: around December 15

But don't rely on the calendar alone. Watch the forecast. If a cold snap is coming, act early. A sudden dip to 40°F can damage tropicals even if frost isn't forecast.

## Which plants need to come in first

Not all plants are equal. Prioritize these:

- **Tropicals**: hibiscus, bougainvillea, mandevilla, plumeria, citrus. They suffer below 50°F.
- **Warm-season annuals**: coleus, begonias, impatiens. They can tolerate 45°F but not frost.
- **Succulents and cacti**: most need to stay above 40°F, but some like agave can handle colder.
- **Hardy perennials in pots**: these can stay out until frost, but the roots are less insulated in pots, so move them if you want to keep them growing.

If you have a mix, bring in the most sensitive first, then the rest over a week.

## The two-week transition plan

Moving plants inside is stressful. Do it gradually to minimize shock.

1. **Inspect for pests**: Check leaves, stems, and soil. Look for aphids, spider mites, whiteflies, and scale. If you see any, treat with insecticidal soap or neem oil before bringing them in. This is the most common mistake: pests hitchhike and infest your indoor plants.
2. **Clean and prune**: Remove dead leaves and spent flowers. Trim back leggy growth. This reduces the plant's energy needs and makes it easier to handle.
3. **Start the transition**: For the first 3 to 4 days, bring the plant inside at night and put it back out during the day. This acclimates it to lower light and humidity.
4. **Move inside permanently**: Place in a bright spot, but not direct sun if it was in shade outside. Gradually increase light over a week.
5. **Adjust watering**: Indoor air is drier, but plants use less water. Check the top inch of soil; water only when dry. Overwatering is the top killer of newly moved plants.

## Ideal indoor conditions

Most houseplants and tropicals need:

- **Light**: 6 to 8 hours of bright, indirect light. A south or west window is best. If you lack light, use a grow light. A basic LED grow light costs $30 to $60 and works well.
- **Humidity**: 40% to 60%. In winter, indoor air can drop to 20%. Use a humidifier or place pots on a tray of pebbles with water.
- **Temperature**: 60°F to 75°F (15°C to 24°C). Avoid drafts from heaters or cold windows.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Bringing in plants with pests**: Always inspect and treat first. One infected plant can spread to your entire collection.
- **Moving from full sun to low light**: This causes leaf drop. Transition gradually.
- **Overwatering**: Plants slow down in lower light. Let the soil dry out more.
- **Fertilizing too soon**: Don't fertilize for the first month. Plants are resting.

## FAQ

**Can I bring in a plant that has already seen frost?**

If it's a tropical, it may be too late. Frost damage can be fatal. If the leaves are black or mushy, the plant may not recover. For hardy plants, it's fine.

**How do I know if my plant has pests before bringing it in?**

Look under leaves and along stems for tiny webs, sticky residue, or small moving dots. Use a magnifying glass if needed. If you see anything, spray with insecticidal soap and repeat after 5 days.

**Do I need to repot before moving inside?**

No. Repotting adds stress. Wait until spring. If the pot is too heavy to move, consider a smaller decorative pot instead.

**What about plants that go dormant?**

Some perennials like cannas or dahlias can be cut back and stored in a cool, dark place. They don't need light. Just keep the soil slightly moist.

## The bottom line

Bring your plants inside when nighttime temperatures stay above 55°F for tropicals, and before the first frost for everything else. Start the transition two weeks early, inspect for pests, and adjust watering. This simple plan keeps your plants healthy through winter and ready to go back out in spring.
