Stardrop tea comes from the leaves of the stardrop plant (Stellaria dropens), a hardy perennial that thrives in USDA zones 4 through 8. It is not a true tea, but a herbal infusion with a mild, sweet flavor and a hint of vanilla. This guide covers how to grow, harvest, and process stardrop leaves into a marketable tea, with realistic costs and yields for a small farm or homestead.

## Getting started: what you need

Stardrop plants prefer well-drained soil with a pH between 6.0 and 7.0. They tolerate partial shade but produce more leaves in full sun. You can start from seeds or transplants. Seeds are slow to germinate, so many growers use 4-inch pots started indoors 8 weeks before the last frost. Transplants cost $3 to $5 each from specialty nurseries, while a packet of 50 seeds runs $8 to $12.

For a small agritourism operation, start with 100 plants. That gives you enough leaves for tastings and a small retail supply. Space plants 18 inches apart in rows 24 inches apart. Prepare the bed by mixing in 2 inches of compost before planting.

## Planting and care

Plant transplants after the last frost date in your area. For zone 5, that is typically mid-May; for zone 7, early April. Water deeply at planting and keep the soil moist for the first month. Once established, stardrop is drought tolerant, but consistent moisture improves leaf quality.

Fertilize in early spring with a balanced organic fertilizer, such as 5-5-5, at a rate of 1 pound per 100 square feet. Do not overdo nitrogen, as it can reduce the aromatic compounds in the leaves. Mulch with straw to suppress weeds and retain moisture.

Stardrop is relatively pest-free. Watch for aphids and treat with insecticidal soap if you see clusters. Deer and rabbits avoid it, which makes it a good crop for edges of a property.

## Harvesting leaves

You can start harvesting in the second year after planting. In the first year, let the plants establish. Harvest leaves just before the plant flowers, usually in late spring to early summer. Cut the top 4 to 6 inches of each stem, including the leaves. This encourages bushier growth and a second, smaller harvest in early fall.

A mature plant yields about 4 to 6 ounces of fresh leaves per harvest. With 100 plants, that is 25 to 37 pounds of fresh leaves per year, which dries down to about 5 to 7 pounds of dried tea. That might sound small, but stardrop tea sells for $30 to $50 per ounce at farm stands and online, so even a small harvest can bring in $150 to $350 per year from 100 plants.

## Processing and drying

Rinse the leaves gently and pat dry. Spread them in a single layer on drying screens or a dehydrator tray. Dry at 95°F to 105°F until the leaves are crisp, usually 12 to 24 hours. You can also air dry in a dark, well-ventilated room for 3 to 5 days, but the flavor is more consistent with a dehydrator.

Once dry, store the leaves in airtight glass jars away from light and heat. Stardrop tea keeps its flavor for up to 18 months. For a finer tea, crumble the leaves by hand or with a food mill, but whole leaves are often preferred for visual appeal.

## Marketing for agritourism

If you have an agritourism operation, offer stardrop tea tastings during farm visits. Brew a pot and let visitors sample it hot or iced. Sell small tins of 1 ounce for $35 to $45. You can also create tea blends with mint or chamomile, which are easy to grow on the same farm.

Consider offering a "tea garden tour" where visitors see the plants and learn about the drying process. Charge $15 per person and include a 0.5-ounce sample to take home. This adds value without much extra labor.

## FAQ

**Can I grow stardrop tea in containers?** Yes, use a 5-gallon pot per plant with good drainage. Container plants need more frequent watering and may produce less, but they work for small spaces or patios.

**How long does stardrop tea take to grow from seed?** Seeds germinate in 2 to 3 weeks indoors, but the plant needs a full year to establish before the first harvest. Transplants save you a season.

**Is stardrop tea the same as regular tea?** No, it is a caffeine-free herbal infusion. It does not come from the Camellia sinensis plant, so it is not a true tea.

**What is the biggest mistake beginners make?** Overwatering. Stardrop roots rot in soggy soil. Let the top inch of soil dry out between waterings, especially in containers.

## The bottom line

Farming stardrop tea is a low-cost, low-maintenance crop that fits well into an agritourism model. Start with 100 plants, harvest in year two, and dry the leaves properly. With a modest investment of $100 to $200 for plants and supplies, you can produce a premium product that sells for $30 to $50 per ounce. It will not replace your main income, but it adds a unique, profitable sideline that visitors will remember.
