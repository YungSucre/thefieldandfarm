Running a pumpkin patch can be a profitable agritourism venture, but it requires careful planning and hard work. Start by choosing the right site, preparing the soil, and selecting varieties that match your growing season and market. This guide covers the essential steps, from planting to selling, with realistic expectations for yields and income.

## Site selection and soil preparation

Pumpkins need full sun (at least 6 hours daily) and well-drained soil with a pH between 6.0 and 6.8. Avoid low spots where water pools; pumpkins rot in wet soil. Test your soil in the fall before planting. Add lime if pH is low, and compost or aged manure to boost organic matter. For a half-acre patch, you might spend $200 to $400 on soil amendments.

Prepare the soil in early spring: till to a depth of 8 to 10 inches, then form raised beds or hills to improve drainage. If you have heavy clay, consider planting on mounds 6 to 8 inches high.

## Choosing pumpkin varieties

Select varieties based on your market: decorative, carving, or pie pumpkins. For a patch, plant a mix to extend the season and attract different customers. Here are proven varieties for US zones 3 to 9:

| Variety | Type | Days to Maturity | Notes |
|---------|------|------------------|-------|
| 'Howden' | Carving | 110-120 | Classic large pumpkin, good handles |
| 'Jack O'Lantern' | Carving | 100-110 | Medium size, reliable |
| 'Sugar Pie' | Pie | 95-100 | Small, sweet flesh |
| 'Baby Bear' | Mini | 95-100 | Small, great for kids |
| 'Cinderella' | Decorative | 100-110 | Ribbed, fairy-tale look |
| 'Atlantic Giant' | Giant | 120-130 | For contests, needs long season |

Plant a mix: 60% carving, 20% pie, 20% decorative or mini. This gives customers variety and spreads your harvest.

## Planting and spacing

Pumpkins are warm-season crops. Plant after the last frost date when soil temperature is at least 60°F. In USDA zones 5 and 6, that's usually mid-May; in zones 7 and 8, early April; in zones 3 and 4, late May to early June. Check your local extension for exact dates.

Sow seeds 1 inch deep. For hills, plant 3 to 4 seeds per hill, then thin to 2 plants per hill. For rows, space seeds 18 to 24 inches apart, then thin to 2 to 3 feet between plants. Rows should be 6 to 8 feet apart for vining types. For bush varieties, you can space rows 4 to 5 feet apart.

Direct sowing is easier than transplanting, but in short-season zones, start seeds indoors 3 to 4 weeks before planting to get a head start.

## Care and maintenance

Water deeply once a week, about 1 to 2 inches, especially during flowering and fruit set. Use drip irrigation or soaker hoses to keep foliage dry and prevent mildew. Mulch around plants to retain moisture and suppress weeds.

Fertilize at planting with a balanced 10-10-10 at a rate of 1 pound per 100 square feet. Side-dress with nitrogen when vines start to run, then switch to a phosphorus-rich fertilizer when flowers appear.

Watch for pests: squash bugs, cucumber beetles, and vine borers. Use row covers early, but remove them when flowers appear to allow pollination. Handpick pests or use insecticidal soap. Powdery mildew is common; plant resistant varieties and ensure good air circulation.

## Harvesting and storage

Pumpkins are ready when the rind is hard and the stem is dry and corky. Cut the stem with pruning shears, leaving 3 to 4 inches attached. Do not carry by the stem; it can break. Cure in the sun for 10 to 14 days to harden the skin, then store in a cool, dry place at 50 to 60°F. Properly cured pumpkins can last 2 to 3 months.

Expect yields of 1,000 to 2,000 pounds per half-acre, depending on variety and care. For example, 'Howden' produces about 1 to 2 pumpkins per plant, each weighing 15 to 25 pounds.

## Pricing and marketing

In 2026, retail prices for pumpkins range from $0.50 to $1.00 per pound for large carving pumpkins, or a flat $5 to $15 each depending on size. Mini pumpkins sell for $1 to $3 each. Pie pumpkins often sell by weight at $0.50 to $0.75 per pound. At a u-pick patch, you might charge $0.60 per pound or $10 per wagon.

To maximize income, add value: sell gourds, corn stalks, hay bales, and mums. Offer a corn maze, hayrides, or a small petting zoo. Many patches charge an entry fee of $5 to $10 per person, which covers the experience, and then charge per pumpkin.

Marketing starts early: create a website or Facebook page, post photos of the growing pumpkins, and announce opening day. Partner with local schools for field trips. Offer weekday discounts to attract families. Use signs on the road and consider a small ad in local papers or on community boards.

## Costs and revenue

Startup costs for a half-acre patch: seeds $50 to $100, soil amendments $200 to $400, irrigation supplies $100 to $300, and marketing $100 to $200. Total initial investment: $450 to $1,000. If you sell 1,500 pumpkins at an average of $6 each, gross revenue is $9,000. Subtract $500 in variable costs (fertilizer, fuel, labor), and net profit could be $8,000, but this varies widely. Many small patches earn $3,000 to $10,000 per season.

## FAQ

**Q: How much land do I need for a pumpkin patch?**
A: A quarter-acre can produce about 500 to 1,000 pumpkins, enough for a small u-pick operation. A full acre can yield 2,000 to 4,000 pumpkins, but requires more labor and marketing.

**Q: Can I grow pumpkins in containers?**
A: Yes, but only bush varieties like 'Sugar Pie' or 'Baby Bear'. Use a 10-gallon container or larger, and expect lower yields. It's not practical for a commercial patch, but fine for a backyard.

**Q: How do I keep animals from eating my pumpkins?**
A: Deer and raccoons can be a problem. Use fencing at least 4 feet high, or use repellents. Harvest promptly and store in a secure area.

**Q: What is the best way to sell pumpkins?**
A: A u-pick model is popular because it reduces labor and gives customers an experience. Alternatively, sell at a farm stand or farmers market. Pre-picked pumpkins sell well if you have a good location.

## The bottom line

Running a pumpkin patch is a rewarding venture that can generate a decent side income, but it requires planning and effort. Start small, choose the right varieties, and market early. With realistic expectations and hard work, you can turn a patch of land into a fall destination. For more details, contact your local extension office for region-specific advice.
