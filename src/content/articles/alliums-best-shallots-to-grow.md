If you want reliable, flavorful shallots, choose varieties that match your climate and storage needs. For most gardeners, 'French Red' and 'Dutch Yellow' are top picks, but 'Gray Shallot' and 'Prisma' offer unique benefits. Plant them in fall or early spring, and you'll harvest bulbs that store for months.

## Why grow shallots?
Shallots (Allium cepa var. aggregatum) are milder and sweeter than onions, and they store well. They take up little space and can be grown in raised beds, containers, or directly in the ground. For homesteaders, they're a high-value crop: a pound of seed sets can yield 8-10 pounds of bulbs.

## Best shallot varieties for different needs

### 1. French Red (also called 'French Red Shallot')
- **Flavor**: Rich, pungent, classic shallot taste.
- **Storage**: Excellent, up to 8 months.
- **Best for**: Cooking, especially sauces and dressings.
- **Growing**: Plant in fall in zones 7-9, early spring in zones 3-6. Space 6 inches apart, 1 inch deep.
- **Yield**: 8-10 bulbs per set.

### 2. Dutch Yellow (also called 'Yellow Shallot')
- **Flavor**: Milder, sweeter than French Red.
- **Storage**: Good, 6 months.
- **Best for**: Raw in salads, roasting.
- **Growing**: Similar to French Red, but more tolerant of cold.
- **Yield**: 6-8 bulbs per set.

### 3. Gray Shallot (also called 'Griselle')
- **Flavor**: Strong, complex, considered the gourmet choice.
- **Storage**: Poor, only 3-4 months, so use first.
- **Best for**: Chefs who want intense flavor.
- **Growing**: Needs well-drained soil, do not overwater. Space 8 inches apart.
- **Yield**: 4-6 bulbs per set.

### 4. Prisma (a hybrid)
- **Flavor**: Mild, sweet.
- **Storage**: Very good, 8 months.
- **Best for**: Long-term storage, mild dishes.
- **Growing**: Vigorous, disease-resistant. Space 6 inches apart.
- **Yield**: 8-10 bulbs per set.

### 5. Conservor (a long-storing variety)
- **Flavor**: Medium, slightly spicy.
- **Storage**: Exceptional, up to 10 months.
- **Best for**: Winter use.
- **Growing**: Plant in spring in cold zones, fall in warm zones.
- **Yield**: 6-8 bulbs per set.

## When to plant shallots by zone
Shallots are usually grown from sets (small bulbs), not seeds, because sets are easier and faster. Here are planting windows:

| USDA Zone | Fall Planting | Spring Planting |
|-----------|---------------|-----------------|
| 3-4       | Not recommended | April, as soon as soil thaws |
| 5-6       | October (if soil is workable) | March-April |
| 7-9       | October-November | February-March |

In zones 3-4, fall planting often fails because the ground freezes hard. Stick to spring planting. In zones 7-9, fall planting gives you larger bulbs next summer.

## How to grow shallots successfully

### Soil preparation
Shallots prefer loose, well-drained soil with a pH of 6.0-7.0. Work in 2 inches of compost before planting. If your soil is heavy clay, consider raised beds.

### Planting steps
1. Separate the sets into individual bulbs.
2. Plant each set pointy end up, 1 inch deep.
3. Space sets 6 inches apart, with rows 12 inches apart.
4. Water well after planting, then keep soil moist but not soggy.

### Care during the season
- **Mulch**: Apply 2 inches of straw or leaves to retain moisture and suppress weeds.
- **Fertilize**: Side-dress with a balanced fertilizer (10-10-10) at 3 weeks and again at 6 weeks, using 1 tablespoon per plant.
- **Water**: About 1 inch per week. Reduce watering as bulbs mature.

### Harvesting and curing
Harvest when the tops yellow and fall over, usually 90-120 days after planting. Lift bulbs gently with a fork. Cure in a warm, dry, airy place for 2-3 weeks. Then trim tops and roots, and store in a cool, dry spot (40-50°F).

## Common problems and solutions
- **Bolting (flowering)**: Caused by cold snaps or stress. Plant at the right time and avoid temperature swings.
- **Rotting**: Usually from poor drainage. Add sand or compost, or use raised beds.
- **Pests**: Onion maggots and thrips. Use row covers and rotate crops.

## Where to buy shallot sets
You can find sets at garden centers or online. Prices range from $10 to $15 per pound for standard varieties, and up to $20 for gourmet types like Gray Shallot. A pound of sets covers about 50 feet of row.

## FAQ

### Can I grow shallots from seed?
Yes, but it takes longer. Seeds started indoors in late winter can be transplanted in spring, but yields are often lower than from sets. It's more economical if you want many plants.

### How many shallots do I get from one set?
Usually 6-10 bulbs, depending on variety and growing conditions.

### Do shallots need full sun?
Yes, at least 6 hours of direct sun daily. More sun means larger bulbs.

### Can I save my own sets for next year?
Yes, save the largest bulbs from your harvest. Store them in a cool, dry place and replant next season. Over time, they may adapt to your local conditions.

## The bottom line
For most gardeners, 'French Red' and 'Dutch Yellow' are the best all-around shallots: reliable, flavorful, and good keepers. If you want gourmet flavor and don't mind shorter storage, try 'Gray Shallot'. For long storage, 'Conservor' is unbeatable. Plant in fall (zones 7-9) or spring (zones 3-6), give them well-drained soil and full sun, and you'll have a steady supply of shallots for your kitchen.
