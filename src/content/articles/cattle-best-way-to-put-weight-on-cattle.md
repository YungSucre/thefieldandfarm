The best way to put weight on cattle is to provide a balanced diet that meets their energy and protein needs, manage parasites, and ensure they have clean water and mineral access. Start by assessing your cattle's current body condition score (BCS) and forage quality, then adjust feed accordingly. This guide covers the key steps, from pasture management to supplemental feeding, with realistic numbers for 2026.

## Assess Your Cattle's Condition
Before changing anything, evaluate each animal's body condition score (BCS) on a 1 to 9 scale. Aim for a BCS of 5 to 6 for mature cows, and 6 to 7 for growing calves or finishing steers. If cattle are below this, they need more energy. Use a simple hands-on check: you shoul

## Forage First: Maximize Pasture Quality
Good pasture is the cheapest feed. In spring and early summer, cool-season grasses like tall fescue or orchardgrass have high protein (18-20%) and energy. In fall, stockpiled fescue can extend grazing. But forage quality drops as it matures, so rotate pastures to keep plants in vegetative stage. For a homestead, a simple rotation of 3-4 paddocks works. Move cattle every 3-5 days in fast growth, every 7-10 days in slower growth. This ensures they eat the top, most nutritious leaves.

If pasture is poor, consider planting a summer annual like pearl millet or sorghum-sudan, which can yield 3-5 tons of dry matter per acre. Seed costs run $20-$40 per acre. But these need nitrogen, so budget for fertilizer: $50-$80 per acre for 50-100 lbs of actual N.

## Supplement Smart: When Forage Isn't Enough
When forage quality drops (typically in mid-summer or winter), you need to supplement. The goal is to meet daily dry matter intake (DMI) of about 2.5% of body weight. For a 1,000 lb steer, that's 25 lbs of dry feed per day.

- **Energy supplements**: Corn, barley, or distillers grains. Corn is the standard: feed 0.5-1% of body weight daily (5-10 lbs for a 1,000 lb animal) to add weight. In 2026, corn prices range $4-$6 per bushel (56 lbs), so $0.07-$0.11 per lb. Distillers grains (wet or dry) are high in protein and energy, often $150-$200 per ton dry matter.
- **Protein supplements**: If forage is low in protein (below 7%), feed soybean meal (48% CP) or a commercial supplement. Soybean meal costs $350-$450 per ton. For a 500 lb calf, 1-2 lbs per day is typical.
- **Complete feed**: For simplicity, a commercial growing ration (e.g., Purina Accuration or Nutrena) costs $250-$350 per ton. Feed 1-2% of body weight daily.

Always introduce grain gradually over 7-10 days to avoid acidosis. Start with 1 lb per day and increase by 1 lb every other day.

## Mineral and Water: The Overlooked Essentials
Cattle need free-choice mineral with a 2:1 calcium to phosphorus ratio, plus trace minerals like copper, zinc, and selenium. A good mineral costs $20-$30 per 50 lb bag, and a cow will consume 2-4 oz per day. Provide fresh, clean water at all times. Cattle drink 10-20 gallons per day, more in heat. If water is limited, feed intake drops, and weight gain suffers.

## Health and Parasite Control
Internal and external parasites rob nutrients. Deworm with an effective product like Ivermectin or Fenbendazole, following label doses. Cost: $2-$5 per dose for injectable, less for pour-on. Also, treat for flies and lice with pour-ons or ear tags. A healthy animal gains weight faster.

## Feeding for Gain: A Sample Ration
For a 700 lb growing steer, targeting 2.5 lbs per day gain, you might feed:
- 15 lbs of good hay (or pasture equivalent)
- 5 lbs of cracked corn
- 1 lb of soybean meal (or 2 lbs of a 20% protein supplement)
- Free-choice mineral and water

This ration provides about 15% protein and 65% TDN (total digestible nutrients). Adjust corn up to 8 lbs if you want 3 lbs gain, but watch for fat deposition.

## Track Progress and Adjust
Weigh cattle every 2-4 weeks if possible. Use a livestock scale (rent for $50-$100 per day) or a weight tape. If gains are below target, increase energy by 1-2 lbs of corn per head per day. If gains are good, maintain. Keep records of feed costs and gains to calculate cost per pound of gain. In 2026, feed costs are higher, so aim for a cost of gain under $1.20 per lb.

## Winter Feeding: Special Considerations
In winter, cattle need more energy to stay warm. Increase hay quality and quantity. For mature cows, feed 30-40 lbs of good hay per day. For growing calves, add grain. Use a hay test to know nutrient content; testing costs $20-$30. If hay is low in protein, supplement with 1-2 lbs of soybean meal.

## FAQ

**How long does it take to put weight on cattle?**
Typically, you can see a noticeable difference in 30-60 days with proper feeding. A growing steer can gain 2-3 lbs per day, so 60-90 lbs in a month. But it depends on starting condition and genetics.

**What is the cheapest way to add weight?**
Good pasture is cheapest. If you must feed, use whole corn or barley, which are often less expensive per pound of energy than commercial feeds. But balance with protein and minerals.

**Can I use kitchen scraps or garden waste?**
Yes, but cautiously. Vegetables like pumpkins or squash can be fed in small amounts (up to 10% of diet). Avoid moldy or spoiled feed. Introduce new feeds gradually.

**How much does it cost to put 100 lbs on a steer?**
With corn at $0.10/lb and a feed conversion of 6:1, you'd need 600 lbs of corn, costing $60. Add hay and mineral, and total might be $80-$120. But if pasture is good, cost drops significantly.

## The bottom line
Putting weight on cattle is about meeting their nutritional needs efficiently. Start with forage, supplement with grain and protein as needed, keep minerals and water available, and control parasites. Monitor body condition and adjust feed based on results. With careful management, you can add 2-3 lbs per day at a reasonable cost. Remember, every animal is different, so observe and adapt. For specific advice, consult your local extension agent or vet.
