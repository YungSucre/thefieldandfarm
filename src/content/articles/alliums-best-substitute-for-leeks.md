Leeks are a garden staple, but they take a long time to mature and can be finicky. If you need a similar onion flavor with less fuss, or you simply want to plant something else this year, here are the best substitutes. Scallions are the closest match for fresh use, while shallots and green garlic work better for cooking. Each has its own growing quirks, so pick based on your zone and how you plan to use them.

## Why substitute leeks?

Leeks are slow growers. They need 120 to 150 days from seed to harvest, and they demand consistent moisture and rich soil. If you have a short season or limited space, a substitute can save you time and effort. Also, leeks are pricey at the store, often $3 to $5 per pound in 2026. Growing a su

## Top substitutes: scallions (green onions)

Scallions are the easiest and fastest substitute. They are immature onions harvested before the bulb forms. The white base and green tops have a mild, fresh onion taste, close to leeks but a bit sharper. Use them raw in salads, stir-fries, or as a garnish.

### Varieties to grow

- 'White Lisbon': a classic, hardy scallion, ready in 60 days.
- 'Evergreen Hardy White': perennial in zones 5-9, very cold tolerant.
- 'Red Beard': a red-stemmed variety, adds color, ready in 65 days.

### Planting and harvest

Sow seeds directly in early spring as soon as soil can be worked, or in late summer for a fall crop. Plant seeds 1/4 inch deep, 1 inch apart in rows 12 inches apart. Thin to 2 inches apart. Harvest when stems are about 1/2 inch thick, usually 60 to 70 days. You can also plant sets (small bulbs) for a faster crop: plant 1 inch deep, 2 inches apart.

Scallions are more cold-hardy than leeks, surviving light frosts. In zone 7 and warmer, you can grow them all winter with a simple row cover.

## Shallots: a richer flavor

Shallots have a milder, sweeter taste than onions, closer to leeks when cooked. They are perfect for sauces, soups, and roasting. They store well, so you can have them for months.

### Varieties to grow

- 'French Red': a classic, reddish-brown skin, spicy flavor, stores well.
- 'Gray Shallot' (griselle): considered the best for flavor, but less productive.
- 'Golden Gourmet': a yellow shallot, sweet and mild, good for beginners.

### Planting and harvest

Plant shallot sets in fall (zones 7-10) or early spring (zones 3-6). Space sets 6 inches apart, 1 inch deep, in rows 12 inches apart. They need full sun and well-drained soil. Harvest when the tops yellow and fall over, usually 90 to 120 days. Cure them in a dry, airy spot for 2 weeks, then store in a cool, dark place.

Shallots are more expensive to buy as sets: about $10 to $15 per pound in 2026, but one pound plants about 50 feet of row. They are more productive than leeks per square foot, yielding 4 to 8 bulbs per set.

## Green garlic: a spring treat

If you have garlic growing, you can harvest it early as a leek substitute. Green garlic is simply immature garlic, pulled before the bulb forms. It has a mild garlic flavor with a hint of sweetness, closer to leeks than mature garlic. Use it in pesto, pasta, or roasted dishes.

### How to grow

Plant garlic cloves in fall, 2 inches deep, 6 inches apart. In early spring, when the plants are about 12 inches tall, you can pull some as green garlic. The flavor is strongest in the white part near the base. You can also plant a dedicated bed in early spring for a summer harvest, but fall planting gives larger plants.

Green garlic is not a direct substitute for leeks in raw dishes, but it works well in cooked recipes. It is a great way to use thinnings from your garlic bed.

## Other options: onion leeks and ramps

- **Onion leeks** (Allium ampeloprasum var. porrum) are a hybrid between onions and leeks. They form a small bulb and have a sweeter flavor. Varieties like 'King Richard' are quick, maturing in 75 days. Plant seeds in early spring, 1/4 inch deep, 2 inches apart.
- **Ramps** (wild leeks) are a woodland allium with a strong garlic-onion flavor. They are not cultivated easily; they need shade and moist soil. If you forage, harvest sustainably, taking only a few leaves per plant. Not recommended for most gardens.

## How to cook with substitutes

When a recipe calls for leeks, you can often swap in an equal amount of scallions or shallots. For raw uses, slice scallions thinly and use the white and light green parts. For cooked dishes, shallots are the best match: chop and sauté them slowly to bring out sweetness. Green garlic is stronger, so use about half the amount and adjust to taste.

If you are substituting in a soup or stew, add the substitute later in cooking to avoid overcooking, since they are more tender than leeks.

## FAQ

**Can I use regular onions as a substitute?**
Yes, but they are stronger. Use a sweet onion like 'Walla Walla' or 'Vidalia' and use about half the amount. For raw dishes, soak sliced onions in cold water for 10 minutes to mellow the bite.

**What is the closest in flavor to leeks?**
Shallots are the closest in cooked dishes, as they are mild and sweet. For raw use, scallions are closer because they have a fresh, mild onion taste.

**Can I grow these substitutes in containers?**
Yes. Scallions and shallots grow well in containers at least 12 inches deep. Use a quality potting mix and ensure good drainage. You can plant scallions every 2 weeks for a continuous harvest.

**Are there any substitutes that store as well as leeks?**
Shallots store much better than leeks. Leeks last a few weeks in the fridge, but shallots can keep for months in a cool, dry place. Scallions are best used fresh, but you can freeze chopped scallions for later use.

## The bottom line

For the easiest and fastest substitute, plant scallions. They are ready in two months and work in most recipes. For a richer flavor and long storage, choose shallots. If you already grow garlic, pull some early for green garlic. Each substitute has its own strengths, so try a couple to see what fits your garden and cooking style. With these options, you can skip leeks and still get that allium goodness.
