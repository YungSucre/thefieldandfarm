Garlic (Allium sativum) and garlic chives (Allium tuberosum) both bring a garlicky punch to the kitchen, but they are very different plants. Garlic grows from a clove into a bulb, harvested once a year. Garlic chives are a perennial herb, grown for their flat leaves and edible flowers, harvested repeatedly. If you want a single, substantial harvest of bulbs for storage, grow garlic. If you want a low-maintenance, cut-and-come-again green for months, grow garlic chives. This guide breaks down the differences so you can choose the right one for your garden.

## Growing requirements

Garlic is a cool-season crop planted in fall (zones 3-8) or early spring (zones 9-10). It needs full sun, well-drained soil, and a cold period to form bulbs. Plant cloves 2 inches deep, 6 inches apart, in rows 12 inches apart. Mulch heavily in cold zones. Harvest in midsummer when lower leaves brown.

Garlic chives are hardy perennials (zones 3-9) that thrive in full sun to partial shade. They tolerate poor soil but prefer rich, moist ground. Start from seed or divisions. Space plants 12 inches apart. They spread by rhizomes and self-seed, so give them room or contain them. Cut leaves as needed from spring to fall; they regrow quickly.

## Flavor and culinary use

Garlic has a strong, pungent flavor that mellows when cooked. Use the bulb in sauces, roasts, and stir-fries. Scapes (the flower stalks) are a spring delicacy, milder than the bulb. Garlic chives have a milder, grassier garlic flavor, with a hint of onion. Use the leaves raw in salads, or cooked in eggs, dumplings, and stir-fries. The white flowers are edible and make a pretty garnish.

## Yield and harvest

Garlic gives one harvest per year. A 10-foot row with 20 plants yields about 20 bulbs, roughly 1 to 2 pounds. Store bulbs for months in a cool, dry place. Garlic chives yield continuously. A single plant can be cut 3 to 5 times per season, producing about 1/2 pound of leaves per plant per year. A 10-foot row of 10 plants yields 5 pounds of leaves over the season. You can also dig and divide plants every few years to increase your patch.

## Cost and effort

Garlic seed cloves cost $8 to $15 per pound, enough for about 50 cloves. Organic seed garlic runs $12 to $20 per pound. Garlic chives seeds cost $2 to $4 per packet (about 100 seeds). Plants or divisions cost $5 to $10 each. Garlic requires more labor: soil prep, planting, mulching, weeding, and curing. Garlic chives are nearly carefree once established, just water during dry spells and cut back in late winter.

## Pest and disease resistance

Garlic is susceptible to fungal diseases like white rot and downy mildew, especially in wet soils. Rotate crops and avoid overhead watering. Garlic chives are rarely bothered by pests or diseases. Deer and rabbits avoid both, making them good choices for rural gardens.

## Which should you grow?

Grow garlic if you want a staple crop for storage and don't mind waiting months for a single harvest. Grow garlic chives if you want a reliable, perennial green that you can snip all season. Many gardeners grow both: garlic for bulbs, garlic chives for fresh greens and as a border plant.

## FAQ

**Can I substitute garlic chives for garlic in recipes?** Not directly. Garlic chives are milder and have a grassy note. Use them where you'd use chives or green onions, not as a replacement for garlic cloves. If a recipe calls for one clove of garlic, use about 1 tablespoon of chopped garlic chives for a milder flavor.

**Do garlic chives spread aggressively?** Yes, they self-seed and spread by rhizomes. In mild climates they can become weedy. Plant them in a contained bed or remove flower heads before they set seed. Divide every 3 years to keep them in check.

**Can I grow garlic chives indoors?** Yes, they grow well in a pot on a sunny windowsill. Use a 6-inch pot with drainage, and cut leaves regularly to encourage new growth. They need at least 6 hours of sun or 12 hours of grow light.

**When is the best time to plant garlic chives?** Plant seeds in early spring or late summer. Divisions can go in the ground in spring or fall. In zones 7 and warmer, fall planting gives a head start.

## The bottom line

Garlic and garlic chives serve different purposes. Garlic is a high-yield, high-effort crop for storage. Garlic chives are a low-effort, continuous green. If you have space, grow both. If you must choose, consider your climate and cooking habits. In cold zones, garlic is a reliable fall-planted crop. In warm zones, garlic chives may be easier because they don't require a cold period. Either way, you'll add a garlicky flavor to your garden and table.
