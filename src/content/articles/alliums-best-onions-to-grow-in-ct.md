If you're gardening in Connecticut, you need onion varieties that handle our cool springs and variable weather. The best onions for CT are short-day and intermediate-day types that bulb before the summer heat peaks, plus a few reliable long-day options if you start early. In this guide, I'll give you specific varieties, planting dates by zone, and the steps to get a solid crop.

## Understanding day length and CT's zones

Onions bulb based on day length. Connecticut spans USDA zones 5b to 7a, with most of the state in 6a/6b. Our summer days reach about 15 hours in June, which is the sweet spot for intermediate-day onions. Short-day onions (12-14 hours) will bulb too early and stay small; long-day onions (14-16 hours) may not bulb fully before the heat slows growth. So focus on intermediate-day types, with a couple of long-day options for early planting.

## When to plant in CT

Onions can be grown from seeds, sets, or transplants. In CT, the timing is critical:

- **Seeds indoors:** Start seeds 8-10 weeks before your last frost date. For most of CT, that's mid-February to early March. Use a seed-starting mix and grow lights. Harden off before transplanting.
- **Sets (small bulbs):** Plant sets directly in the garden 2-4 weeks before your last frost, usually late March to mid-April. Sets are easier for beginners but may bolt if they're large.
- **Transplants:** Buy or grow transplants and set them out in early April, once the soil can be worked. Transplants give the best bulb size.

Last frost dates: zone 5b around May 10, zone 6a around April 30, zone 6b around April 20, zone 7a around April 10. Adjust accordingly.

## Soil prep and planting steps

Onions need loose, well-drained soil with a pH of 6.0-6.8. Here's what to do:

1. **Test your soil** (a simple kit is $10-15). Add lime if pH is low, or sulfur if high.
2. **Amend with compost** (2-3 inches) and a balanced fertilizer like 10-10-10 at 1 pound per 10 square feet.
3. **Plant sets or transplants** 1 inch deep, spacing 4-6 inches apart in rows 12-18 inches apart. For larger bulbs, go 6 inches.
4. **Water well** after planting, then keep soil consistently moist (about 1 inch per week).
5. **Mulch** with straw or grass clippings to retain moisture and suppress weeds.

If you're planting seeds directly (not recommended for CT), sow 1/2 inch deep in early April, then thin to 4 inches apart.

## Care and common problems

- **Fertilize** every 3-4 weeks with a high-nitrogen fertilizer (like blood meal or 21-0-0) until bulbs start to swell. Stop once the necks soften.
- **Weeds** are the biggest enemy. Hand-pull or hoe shallowly, as onion roots are shallow.
- **Pests:** Onion maggots can be an issue. Use row covers at planting, and rotate crops. Thrips are less common in CT but treat with insecticidal soap if you see silvery streaks.
- **Diseases:** Avoid overhead watering to prevent fungal rot. Ensure good air circulation.

## Harvesting and storing

Harvest when tops flop over and start to yellow, usually mid-July to August. Pull onions on a dry day, then cure in a warm, dry, airy spot for 2-3 weeks. Cut tops to 1 inch and roots, then store in a cool, dry place (40-50°F). Short-day onions store only a few months, so use them first. Long-day storage types can last through winter if cured well.

## Cost and yield expectations

A packet of seeds costs $3-5 and yields hundreds of plants. A pound of sets costs $8-12 and covers about 50 feet. Expect 10-15 pounds of onions per 10-foot row with proper spacing. In 2026, prices for transplants run $10-15 for a bundle of 50-100.

## FAQ

**Can I grow onions from seeds in CT?** Yes, but start them indoors in February. Direct seeding in April often results in small bulbs because the season is too short. Transplants or sets are more reliable.

**What's the best onion for sweet flavor in CT?** 'Candy' and 'Super Star' are both sweet and mild. They don't store long, so eat them fresh.

**Why are my onions bolting?** Bolting (flowering) happens when plants are exposed to cold (below 50°F) for several weeks after being large enough. Avoid planting large sets too early, and use varieties less prone to bolting like 'Candy'.

**How do I know when to harvest?** When about half the tops have flopped over, bend the rest down, then wait a few days. Pull them all within a week to prevent regrowth.

## The bottom line

For Connecticut, stick to intermediate-day onions like 'Candy', 'Red Candy Apple', and 'Talon'. Plant sets or transplants in early April, give them loose soil and steady moisture, and you'll have a solid harvest by August. If you want storage onions, add 'Copra' or 'Patterson' for northern zones. Start small, keep notes, and adjust next year. Onions are forgiving if you get the basics right.
