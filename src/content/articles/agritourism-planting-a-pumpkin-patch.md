Planting a pumpkin patch starts with choosing the right variety for your climate and space, then preparing soil with compost and fertilizer, and sowing seeds after the last frost. For most zones, that means planting between late May and early July, depending on your region. This guide covers the exact steps, from soil prep to harvest, with realistic numbers for 2026.

## Choose the right pumpkin variety

Pumpkins fall into three main categories: carving, pie, and decorative. Each has different spacing and days to maturity.

- **Carving pumpkins**: 'Howden Biggie' (110 days), 'Connecticut Field' (110 days), 'Jack-O'-Lantern' (100 days). These need 4-6 feet between hills.
- **Pie pumpkins**: 'Sugar Pie' (100 days), 'New England Pie' (105 days), 'Small Sugar' (100 days). These are smaller and can be planted 3-4 feet apart.
- **Decorative**: 'Baby Boo' (90 days), 'Jack-Be-Little' (95 days), 'Cinderella' (100 days). These are often grown on trellises to save space.

For a patch, choose one or two varieties. If you want both carving and pie, plant them in separate areas to avoid cross-pollination, which can affect flavor and appearance.

## When to plant: timing by USDA zone

Pumpkins are warm-season crops. They need soil temperature of at least 60°F (16°C) to germinate, and they are killed by frost. The key is to plant so that the pumpkins mature before the first fall frost, but not so early that they rot in summer heat.

In zones 3-5, you may need to start seeds indoors 2-3 weeks before the last frost, then transplant. In zones 6-9, direct sow is usually fine. For example, in zone 5, plant seeds outdoors around May 20, or start indoors May 1 and transplant June 1.

## Site selection and soil preparation

Pumpkins need full sun (at least 6-8 hours), well-drained soil, and plenty of space. They are heavy feeders, so soil prep is critical.

1. **Test your soil**: A simple soil test costs $15-$30 at a local extension office. Aim for pH 6.0-6.8.
2. **Clear the area**: Remove weeds and grass. If you're using a new patch, consider solarization or tilling.
3. **Amend with compost**: Spread 2-3 inches of compost over the planting area and till it in. This adds organic matter and nutrients.
4. **Add fertilizer**: Before planting, work in a balanced fertilizer like 10-10-10 at a rate of 2-3 pounds per 100 square feet. Or use a pumpkin-specific fertilizer, which costs about $15-$25 per bag.

If you're planting in rows, space hills 4-6 feet apart in rows 8-10 feet apart. For hills, make a mound of soil about 12 inches high and 2 feet wide. This improves drainage and warms the soil faster.

## How to plant: seeds vs. transplants

Direct sowing is the most reliable method for pumpkins because they resent root disturbance. But in short-season zones, transplants give you a head start.

**Direct sowing**:
- Plant 2-3 seeds per hill, 1 inch deep.
- After seedlings emerge, thin to 1-2 plants per hill, keeping the strongest.
- Water gently but thoroughly after planting.

**Transplants**:
- Start seeds in peat pots 2-3 weeks before your planting date.
- Use a seed-starting mix, keep soil at 70-80°F.
- Harden off for 7-10 days before transplanting.
- Transplant on a cloudy day or in the evening to reduce shock.

## Watering and fertilizing through the season

Pumpkins need consistent moisture, especially during flowering and fruit set. Water deeply once or twice a week, providing 1-2 inches of water per week. Avoid overhead watering in the evening to prevent mildew.

Fertilize again when vines start to run (about 4 weeks after planting) with a high-nitrogen fertilizer like 20-10-10, at a rate of 1 pound per 100 square feet. When flowers appear, switch to a high-phosphorus fertilizer like 5-15-5 to encourage fruit development.

Mulch around plants with straw or black plastic to retain moisture and suppress weeds. This also keeps pumpkins clean and reduces rot.

## Managing pests and diseases

Common problems include squash bugs, cucumber beetles, and powdery mildew. Here's how to handle them without resorting to harsh chemicals.

- **Squash bugs**: Handpick eggs and adults in the morning. Use row covers until flowering, then remove to allow pollination.
- **Cucumber beetles**: Use floating row covers or apply neem oil. Yellow sticky traps can help.
- **Powdery mildew**: Space plants properly for air circulation, water at the base, and apply a sulfur-based fungicide if needed.

## Harvesting and storing

Pumpkins are ready when the rind is hard and the stem has dried and turned brown. Cut the stem with a sharp knife, leaving 3-4 inches attached. Do not carry pumpkins by the stem; they can break.

Cure pumpkins in the sun for 10-14 days to harden the skin, then store in a cool, dry place (50-60°F) with good air circulation. Properly cured pumpkins can last 2-3 months.

## Costs and yields for 2026

Starting a pumpkin patch costs about $50-$150 for seeds, fertilizer, and soil amendments for a 100-foot row. Seeds cost $2-$5 per packet (about 20-30 seeds). If you need more, bulk seeds run $10-$20 per ounce.

Yields vary by variety and care. A typical carving pumpkin vine produces 1-2 pumpkins, each weighing 15-25 pounds. Pie pumpkins produce 3-5 per vine, each 2-4 pounds. So a 100-foot row with 20 hills could yield 20-40 carving pumpkins or 60-100 pie pumpkins.

## FAQ

**Can I plant pumpkins in containers?**
Yes, but choose small varieties like 'Baby Boo' or 'Jack-Be-Little'. Use a 10-15 gallon container per plant, and provide a trellis. Expect lower yields, about 1-2 pumpkins per plant.

**How long do pumpkins take to grow?**
Most varieties take 90-120 days from seed to harvest. Check the days to maturity on the seed packet and count backward from your first frost date.

**Why are my pumpkin flowers falling off?**
Pumpkins have male and female flowers. Male flowers appear first and fall off naturally. If female flowers (with a small fruit at the base) fall off, it's likely poor pollination. Hand-pollinate by transferring pollen from male to female flowers with a cotton swab.

**Should I rotate pumpkin crops?**
Yes, rotate pumpkins with other crops every 3-4 years to reduce soil-borne diseases. Avoid planting them where squash or melons grew the previous year.

## The bottom line

Planting a pumpkin patch is straightforward if you choose the right variety, plant at the right time for your zone, and give the vines room to spread. Prepare your soil with compost and fertilizer, water consistently, and watch for pests. With a little effort, you'll have pumpkins for carving, pies, or selling at a farm stand. Start with a small patch of 10-20 hills to learn the ropes, then expand next year.
