Plant ageratum after the last frost, but start seeds indoors 6 to 8 weeks earlier. For most zones, that means sowing indoors in late March or early April, and transplanting outdoors in mid to late May. Ageratum is a tender annual, so any frost will kill it. If you want direct sowing, wait until soil temperatures reach 60°F, which usually happens a week or two after the last frost date.

## Why timing matters

Ageratum (Ageratum houstonianum) thrives in warm soil and air. Planting too early stunts growth or causes rot. Planting too late means fewer blooms before fall frost. The goal is to give plants 8 to 10 weeks of growth before they go in the ground, so they establish roots and start flowering by early summer.

## Indoor planting dates by USDA zone

Start seeds indoors 6 to 8 weeks before your average last frost date. Use a seed-starting mix, not garden soil. Press seeds lightly into the surface, as they need light to germinate. Keep the mix at 70°F to 75°F. A heat mat helps, but a warm room works too. Seeds germinate in 7 to 14 days.

These dates are averages. Check your local frost dates with your extension office or an online almanac. If you are in a microclimate, adjust by a week or two.

## Direct sowing outdoors

You can sow ageratum seeds directly in the garden, but they will bloom later, about 8 to 10 weeks after sowing. Wait until the soil is at least 60°F. In most zones, that is 1 to 2 weeks after the last frost. Sow seeds 1/8 inch deep, spaced 6 to 8 inches apart. Thin seedlings to 8 to 12 inches apart once they have two sets of true leaves. Direct sowing works best in zones 7 and warmer, where the season is long enough for a good bloom period.

## Transplanting steps

1. Harden off seedlings for 7 to 10 days before transplanting. Set them outside in a sheltered spot for 1 hour on day one, then increase by 1 hour each day. This prevents shock.
2. Choose a site with full sun (at least 6 hours) and well-drained soil. Ageratum tolerates partial shade, but blooms are fewer.
3. Space plants 8 to 12 inches apart. Closer spacing gives a denser look, but reduces air circulation.
4. Dig holes slightly larger than the root ball. Place the plant at the same depth it was in the pot. Firm the soil gently.
5. Water thoroughly after planting. Keep the soil evenly moist for the first week.
6. Add a layer of organic mulch, like straw or shredded bark, to retain moisture and suppress weeds.

## Varieties and their timing

Choose varieties based on your bloom preference and season length. Dwarf types are great for borders, while taller ones work for cut flowers.

## Tools and costs

You do not need fancy equipment. A basic seed-starting kit costs $20 to $40. A heat mat adds $15 to $25. A soil thermometer costs $10 to $15. If you buy transplants, expect to pay $3 to $5 per 4-inch pot, or $10 to $15 for a 6-pack. Seeds are cheaper: a packet of 100 seeds costs $3 to $6, and one packet covers a 10-foot row.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Planting too early: Ageratum is frost tender. Even a light frost kills it. Wait until nighttime temperatures stay above 50°F.
- Overwatering: Ageratum prefers consistent moisture but not soggy soil. Water when the top inch of soil is dry.
- Skipping hardening off: Transplants that go straight from indoors to full sun often scorch. Always harden off.
- Using garden soil for seeds: It compacts and may harbor diseases. Use a sterile seed-starting mix.

## FAQ

**Can I plant ageratum in fall?** Yes, in zones 9 and warmer, you can plant in fall for winter blooms. Start seeds in late summer (August) and transplant in September or October. In cooler zones, fall planting is not practical because frost kills the plants.

**How long does ageratum take to bloom from seed?** From indoor sowing, expect blooms 10 to 12 weeks after starting. From direct sowing, add 2 to 3 weeks. So if you start indoors in early April, you will see flowers by mid-June.

**Does ageratum need full sun?** It does best in full sun, but it tolerates light shade, especially in hot climates. In zones 8 and warmer, afternoon shade can extend bloom life.

**Can I save seeds from my ageratum?** Yes, but note that many hybrids do not come true from seed. If you want to save seeds, choose open-pollinated varieties like 'Blue Horizon'. Collect dried flower heads in late summer, remove the seeds, and store in a cool, dry place.

## The bottom line

Plant ageratum after the last frost, but start seeds indoors 6 to 8 weeks earlier for the longest bloom season. Match your dates to your USDA zone, harden off transplants, and space them 8 to 12 inches apart. With the right timing, you will have fluffy blue, white, or purple blooms from early summer until frost. Ageratum is a low-maintenance annual that rewards a little planning with months of color.
