Farmers plant crops based on two hard rules: soil temperature and frost dates. Plant too early and seeds rot; plant too late and heat or frost kills the crop. For most of the US, the window runs from late February (Zone 9) to early July (Zone 3 for fall crops). This guide gives you the exact dates, soil temps, and varieties that work, so you can plan your planting week by week.

## Know your zone and your last frost date

Your USDA hardiness zone sets the calendar. Find your zone at planthardiness.ars.usda.gov. Then look up your average last spring frost date (search "last frost date [your city]"). This date is your anchor: cool-season crops go in 2 to 4 weeks before it, warm-season crops 1 to 2 weeks after.

For example, in Zone 5 (Chicago), last frost is around April 20. In Zone 7 (Nashville), it is April 5. In Zone 9 (Houston), it is February 15. Adjust all dates below to your zone.

## Cool-season crops: plant early, harvest before heat

Cool-season crops tolerate frost and grow best in 50 to 65°F soil. They include lettuce, spinach, peas, carrots, beets, broccoli, and cabbage. Plant them as soon as the soil can be worked, even if snow is still possible.

| Crop | Soil temp (F) | Planting window (Zone 5) | Planting window (Zone 7) |
|------|---------------|--------------------------|--------------------------|
| Peas | 40-50 | March 20 - April 10 | March 1 - March 20 |
| Lettuce | 40-75 | April 1 - April 20 | March 10 - March 30 |
| Carrots | 40-85 | April 1 - April 20 | March 10 - March 30 |
| Broccoli (transplants) | 50-60 | April 1 - April 15 | March 15 - April 1 |
| Spinach | 35-50 | March 20 - April 10 | March 1 - March 20 |

For a fall harvest, plant these again 8 to 10 weeks before your first fall frost (around September 1 in Zone 5). Use varieties like 'Waltham 29' broccoli, 'Danvers 126' carrots, and 'Bloomsdale' spinach.

## Warm-season crops: wait for soil to hit 60°F

Warm-season crops (tomatoes, peppers, corn, beans, squash) die at 32°F and stop growing below 50°F. Plant them only after the soil reaches 60°F and the frost risk is low. In Zone 5, that is May 15 to June 1. In Zone 7, it is April 15 to May 1. In Zone 9, it is March 1 to April 1.

| Crop | Soil temp (F) | Planting window (Zone 5) | Planting window (Zone 7) |
|------|---------------|--------------------------|--------------------------|
| Tomatoes (transplants) | 60-70 | May 15 - June 1 | April 15 - May 1 |
| Sweet corn | 55-65 | May 15 - June 1 | April 15 - May 1 |
| Green beans | 60-85 | May 15 - June 1 | April 15 - May 1 |
| Summer squash | 60-75 | May 15 - June 1 | April 15 - May 1 |
| Peppers (transplants) | 65-75 | May 20 - June 5 | April 20 - May 5 |

Use a soil thermometer ($10 to $15 at garden centers) to check at 2 inches deep, in the morning. If it is below 60°F, wait. For tomatoes, choose early varieties like 'Early Girl' (60 days) or 'Celebrity' (70 days) for northern zones. For corn, 'Golden Bantam' (75 days) works in short seasons.

## How to read seed packets and use soil temperature

Seed packets list "days to maturity" and often a planting range like "after last frost." But the real rule is soil temperature. For example, beans germinate at 60°F, but at 50°F they rot. Use a simple rule: if the soil is too cold to sit on comfortably, it is too cold for warm-season seeds.

To speed up soil warming, cover beds with black plastic or row cover a week before planting. This can raise soil temperature by 5 to 10°F, letting you plant a week earlier.

## Tools and costs for accurate planting

You do not need expensive gear. A basic kit costs under $50:

- Soil thermometer: $10 to $15
- Garden trowel: $8 to $12
- Row cover or frost cloth: $15 to $25 for a 6x50 foot roll
- Seed packets: $3 to $5 each, enough for 50 to 100 plants

For larger fields, a seed drill or walk-behind planter costs $200 to $500 used, but for a home garden, hand planting works fine.

## Adjust for your microclimate

Your local weather can shift dates by 2 weeks. Check the 10-day forecast before planting. If a frost is coming, cover young plants with row cover or a sheet. If a heat wave hits, water deeply and mulch to keep soil cool.

## FAQ

**Can I plant before the last frost date?**
Yes, for cool-season crops. They tolerate light frost (down to 28°F for peas and spinach). For warm-season crops, wait until after the last frost, or use row cover for protection.

**What if I miss the planting window?**
For most crops, you can plant 1 to 2 weeks late and still get a harvest, but yields drop. For fall crops, you can plant in late summer. For tomatoes, choose a shorter-season variety.

**Do I need to start seeds indoors?**
Only for long-season crops like tomatoes, peppers, and broccoli. Start them 6 to 8 weeks before your last frost. For direct-sown crops like beans and carrots, plant seeds straight in the ground.

**How do I know if the soil is ready?**
Grab a handful of soil and squeeze. If it crumbles, it is ready. If it forms a muddy ball, wait a few days. Also, check the soil temperature with a thermometer.

## The bottom line

Plant cool-season crops 2 to 4 weeks before your last frost, and warm-season crops 1 to 2 weeks after, when soil hits 60°F. Use a soil thermometer, check your zone's frost dates, and choose varieties matched to your season length. Start with peas and lettuce in early spring, then add tomatoes and beans after the frost. With these dates, you will avoid the two biggest mistakes: planting too early and planting too late.
