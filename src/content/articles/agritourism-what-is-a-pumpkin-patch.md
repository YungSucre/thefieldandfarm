A pumpkin patch is a designated field where pumpkins are grown for sale, often as a pick-your-own experience for families and schools. For gardeners and homesteaders, it can be a profitable agritourism venture or a simple backyard crop. This guide covers the essentials: what it takes to start, realistic costs, and how to make it work in 2026.

## The basics of a pumpkin patch

A pumpkin patch is more than just a field of orange gourds. It is a managed growing area, typically 0.5 to 5 acres, where you plant pumpkins in rows or hills, care for them through the season, and harvest in late summer to fall. The term also refers to the retail side: many farms open their patch to visitors for pick-your-own, hayrides, and corn mazes. Even a small backyard patch can supply your family and neighbors, but if you want to sell, you need scale and planning.

## Why grow pumpkins?

Pumpkins are relatively easy to grow, have a long shelf life (2 to 3 months if cured), and command good prices in the fall. In 2026, wholesale prices range from $0.30 to $0.60 per pound, while retail pick-your-own prices are $0.50 to $1.00 per pound, or $5 to $15 per pumpkin depending on size. A well-managed acre can yield 1,000 to 2,000 pumpkins, so gross revenue can be $3,000 to $10,000 per acre, but costs and labor eat into that. For agritourism, you can charge admission ($8 to $15 per person) and sell extras like cider and gourds, which boosts profit.

## Getting started: site and soil

Pumpkins need full sun (at least 6 to 8 hours), well-drained soil, and a pH between 6.0 and 6.8. Test your soil before planting; amend with compost or aged manure. Avoid low spots where water pools, as pumpkins rot in wet conditions. If you are on a small homestead, a 50 by 50 foot plot (about 0.06 acre) can produce 50 to 100 pumpkins, enough for a family and a few sales.

## Choosing varieties

Pick varieties based on your market. For carving, classic orange types like 'Howden' or 'Connecticut Field' are reliable. For painting and decorating, white 'Casper' or 'Lumina' and mini 'Jack Be Little' are popular. For pies, 'Sugar Pie' or 'New England Pie' are sweet and dense. For giant pumpkins, 'Atlantic Giant' can reach 500 pounds, but they need extra care and space. In 2026, seed prices are $2 to $5 per packet, or $50 to $150 per pound for bulk seed.

## Planting and care

Plant after the last frost, when soil temperature is at least 60°F. In USDA zones 3 to 5, that is late May to early June; zones 6 to 8, mid-April to May; zones 9 to 10, February to March. Sow seeds 1 inch deep, 2 to 3 seeds per hill, spacing hills 4 to 8 feet apart in rows 6 to 10 feet apart. Thin to the strongest plant per hill. Pumpkins need 1 to 2 inches of water per week, more in heat. Use drip irrigation or soaker hoses to keep leaves dry and prevent mildew. Fertilize with a balanced 10-10-10 at planting and again when vines start to run. Watch for squash bugs and vine borers; use row covers early and remove when flowers appear.

## Harvest and storage

Pumpkins are ready when the rind is hard and the stem is dry and corky. Cut with a sharp knife, leaving 3 to 4 inches of stem. Do not carry by the stem; it can break. Cure in the sun for 10 to 14 days to harden the skin, then store in a cool, dry place at 50 to 60°F. Properly cured, they last 2 to 3 months. For a fall market, plant so harvest peaks in late September to October.

## Turning it into a business

If you want to sell, start small. In your first year, plant a quarter acre and sell at a roadside stand or farmers market. In 2026, a simple stand costs $200 to $500 for tables, signage, and a cash box. You will need liability insurance ($300 to $800 per year) and a business license (varies by state). For agritourism, add activities like a hay bale maze or a small corn maze, but these require extra land and time. Many farms charge $10 to $15 per person for access, which covers the pumpkin as a take-home.

## Marketing your patch

Use social media to announce opening day, post photos of the vines, and share harvest updates. Partner with local schools for field trips, offering a discount for groups. In 2026, a simple website or Facebook page is enough to start. Word of mouth is powerful: ask happy customers to leave reviews. Price competitively: check local farms and set your rates 10% lower if you are new.

## Common mistakes to avoid

Do not plant too early; frost kills vines. Do not overwater, which causes rot. Do not skip pest control; squash bugs can decimate a patch. Do not harvest too early; immature pumpkins do not store. Finally, do not underestimate labor: weeding, watering, and harvesting take time. A quarter acre can require 10 to 20 hours per week in peak season.

## FAQ

**How much land do I need for a pumpkin patch?**
For a family, 50 by 50 feet is enough. For a small business, start with a quarter acre, which can yield 250 to 500 pumpkins. An acre is a full-time commitment.

**What is the best pumpkin variety for beginners?**
'Howden' is forgiving and produces classic carving pumpkins. 'Sugar Pie' is easy for pies. Both are widely available and disease resistant.

**How long do pumpkins take to grow?**
Most varieties mature in 90 to 120 days. Check the seed packet for days to maturity and count back from your first frost date.

**Can I grow pumpkins in containers?**
Yes, but only small varieties like 'Jack Be Little' in 10 to 15 gallon pots. Full-size pumpkins need ground space for vines.

## The bottom line

A pumpkin patch is a rewarding project for gardeners and a viable small business for homesteaders. Start small, choose the right varieties, and plan for the fall market. With realistic expectations and careful management, you can grow pumpkins that feed your family and bring in extra income. The key is to prepare the soil, plant at the right time, and manage pests. Then enjoy the harvest and the smiles of visitors who pick their own.
