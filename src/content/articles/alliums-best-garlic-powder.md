## Why homemade garlic powder wins

Store-bought garlic powder is often made from older, lower-grade garlic and may include additives like silicon dioxide to prevent caking. Homemade powder uses fresh, high-quality garlic, so it has a stronger, sweeter, more complex flavor. You also control the grind: a fine powder for rubs, a coarser grind for sprinkling. ## How to make garlic powder at home

Making garlic powder is simple but requires patience. Here is the process:

1. **Peel and slice**: Peel 1 to 2 pounds of garlic cloves. Slice them thinly, about 1/8 inch thick. A mandoline makes this fast, but a sharp knife works.
2. **Dry**: The key is to dry the slices completely. Use a dehydrator at 115°F for 8 to 12 hours, or an oven set to its lowest temperature (usually 170°F) with the door cracked, for 4 to 6 hours. Slices should snap, not bend, when done.
3. **Grind**: Pulverize the dried slices in a spice grinder or high-speed blender. Sift through a fine-mesh strainer to get a uniform powder. Return larger bits to the grinder.
4. **Store**: Keep in an airtight jar in a cool, dark place. It will last up to a year, but flavor fades after 6 months.

**Yield**: 1 pound of fresh garlic yields about 3 to 4 ounces of powder. So a typical 50-bulb harvest (about 5 pounds) gives you roughly 1 pound of powder, enough for a year of cooking.

**Cost**: If you grow your own, the cost is just your time and a little electricity (about $2 for dehydrator use). If you buy garlic at $2 per pound, homemade powder costs about $16 per pound, still less than most premium store brands.

## Garlic powder vs. granulated vs. garlic salt

Know the difference before you buy:

- **Garlic powder**: Finely ground, dissolves quickly, good for sauces and dressings.
- **Granulated garlic**: Coarser, takes longer to release flavor, better for dry rubs and marinades.
- **Garlic salt**: Garlic powder mixed with salt, often with anti-caking agents. Use only if you adjust your recipe's salt. It is not a substitute for pure garlic powder.

For most cooking, granulated garlic is more versatile because it does not clump and has a more robust texture. But for soups or smooth sauces, powder is better.

## How to use garlic powder effectively

Garlic powder is not a one-to-one substitute for fresh garlic. Use these ratios:

- 1 clove fresh = 1/4 teaspoon garlic powder = 1/2 teaspoon granulated garlic.
- Add powder early in cooking to let it rehydrate and mellow. If added at the end, it can taste harsh.
- Toast powder in a dry pan for 30 seconds to deepen flavor before adding liquids.

## Storing garlic powder

Store in an airtight container away from heat, light, and moisture. A cool pantry is fine. Do not refrigerate, as condensation can cause clumping. Properly stored, powder keeps for a year, but for best flavor, use within 6 months. If it clumps or loses aroma, it is past its prime.

## FAQ

**Is homemade garlic powder safe?**
Yes, if you dry the garlic thoroughly to a moisture content below 10%. Dehydrating at 115°F or higher kills pathogens. Store in a dry place to prevent mold.

**Can I use a microwave to dry garlic?**
No. Microwaves cook unevenly and can burn garlic. Use a dehydrator or oven for consistent results.

**Why is my homemade garlic powder bitter?**
Bitterness usually comes from overheating during drying or from using old, sprouting garlic. Dry at low temperature and use fresh bulbs.

**How long does homemade garlic powder last?**
Up to a year in an airtight jar in a cool, dark place. After 6 months, flavor declines, so make small batches.

## The bottom line

The best garlic powder is the one you make from your own harvest. It tastes better, costs less, and gives you control over quality. If you buy, choose a brand with no additives and a strong aroma. For most uses, granulated garlic is more versatile than powder. Start with a small batch this season, and you will never go back to store-bought.
