Managing a farm, even a small one, comes down to three things: planning, daily routines, and honest record keeping. You do not need a degree in agribusiness, but you do need a system. This guide gives you a concrete framework to manage your farm this season, with real numbers and tools that work in 2026.

## Start with a farm plan

A farm plan is your roadmap. It does not have to be fancy, but it must answer four questions: What are you growing or raising? Where will it go? When will you plant or breed? How will you sell or use it?

For a mixed homestead, keep it simple. List each crop or animal, the space it needs, and the expected yield. For example, a 100-foot row of green beans yields about 50 pounds. A laying hen produces 4 to 5 eggs per week. Write these numbers down. They become your budget and your schedule.

Use a paper notebook or a spreadsheet. I use a simple Google Sheets template with columns for task, date, cost, and notes. It takes 15 minutes a week to update. That habit saves you from surprises like planting too late or overspending on feed.

## Daily and weekly routines

A farm runs on routines. In the growing season, plan for 1 to 2 hours of chores per day for a half-acre homestead. Here is a realistic breakdown:

- Morning (30-45 minutes): water seedlings, check livestock, collect eggs, move electric fence if needed.
- Evening (30 minutes): harvest what is ready, water again if hot, close coops, check for pests.
- Weekly (2-3 hours): weed, mulch, repair fences, clean waterers, update records.

For livestock, daily checks are non-negotiable. Look for signs of illness: lethargy, runny nose, or reduced appetite. Catch problems early. A sick chicken costs $5 to treat, but a dead one costs $20 to replace.

## Manage your finances

Farming is a business, even if you only sell at a farmers market. Track every dollar. In 2026, expect these costs (US averages):

- Seeds: $3 to $6 per packet, or $50 to $100 for a full garden.
- Chicken feed: $20 to $30 per 50-pound bag, feeding 10 hens for about 3 weeks.
- Fencing: $1 to $2 per foot for electric netting.
- Soil test: $15 to $30 per sample.

Income varies. A dozen eggs sells for $4 to $7 at a farm stand. A 10-pound box of mixed vegetables brings $20 to $30. Do not expect to get rich. A small farm might net $2,000 to $5,000 per year after expenses, if you sell consistently.

Keep a simple profit and loss sheet. List income on one side, expenses on the other. Update it monthly. If a crop costs more to grow than it returns, cut it or raise the price.

## Keep records that matter

Record keeping is the most skipped chore, but it is what separates a hobby from a farm. You need three records:

1. Planting log: date, variety, location, days to harvest.
2. Livestock log: births, deaths, feed purchases, health treatments.
3. Financial log: all income and expenses.

Use a three-ring binder or a digital app like Farmbrite (free tier for small farms). Write down what worked and what failed. For example, note that 'Green Mountain' potatoes did poorly in clay soil, but 'Yukon Gold' thrived. Next year, you adjust.

## Plan for the seasons

Seasonal planning prevents chaos. Here is a month-by-month checklist for USDA zones 5-7 (adjust for your zone):

| Month | Tasks |
|-------|-------|
| January | Order seeds, plan crop rotation, repair tools |
| February | Start indoor seedlings (peppers, tomatoes), prune fruit trees |
| March | Direct sow peas, spinach, lettuce; set up drip irrigation |
| April | Plant potatoes, onions, carrots; move chicks to brooder |
| May | Transplant tomatoes, squash; mulch; start summer squash |
| June | Harvest greens, peas; plant beans, corn; monitor pests |
| July | Harvest early tomatoes, zucchini; water deeply; plant fall brassicas |
| August | Harvest onions, garlic; plant fall lettuce, kale; freeze excess |
| September | Harvest winter squash, potatoes; plant cover crops |
| October | Clean up beds, add compost; process meat birds |
| November | Store root crops; winterize irrigation; order next year's seed |
| December | Review records, plan next year, repair fences |

This is not exhaustive, but it gives you a rhythm. Adjust dates based on your local frost dates.

## Handle common problems

Problems will happen. Here is how to deal with the big three:

- Pests: Use row covers for insects. For deer, a 6-foot fence or motion-activated sprinkler works. For weeds, mulch with straw or cardboard. Do not use chemicals unless absolutely necessary.
- Disease: Rotate crops each year. Do not plant tomatoes in the same spot more than once every 3 years. Choose disease-resistant varieties like 'Defiant' tomato or 'Marketmore' cucumber.
- Equipment failure: Keep a basic repair kit: duct tape, zip ties, a multi-tool, and spare parts for your irrigation system. A $20 kit saves you a $100 service call.

## Use the right tools

You do not need a tractor. For a small farm, these tools are essential and cost-effective:

- Hori hori knife ($25-$40): for digging, weeding, and dividing plants.
- Drip irrigation kit ($50-$100 for 100 feet): saves water and time.
- Wheel hoe ($100-$150): for weeding between rows.
- Digital kitchen scale ($15-$25): for weighing harvests and feed.
- Soil thermometer ($10-$15): to know when to plant.

Buy quality once. A $100 wheel hoe lasts 10 years; a $40 one breaks in 2.

## FAQ

**How much time does farm management take per week?**
Expect 7 to 10 hours per week for a half-acre homestead during the growing season. In winter, drop to 2 to 3 hours for animal care and planning.

**What is the best software for farm records?**
For most small farms, a spreadsheet is enough. If you want a dedicated tool, Farmbrite has a free plan for up to 50 animals and 100 crops. Or use a paper planner if you prefer analog.

**How do I know if my farm is profitable?**
Track all costs and income for one full year. If your income covers your expenses, you are breaking even. Most small farms do not profit in year one. Aim for a positive margin by year three.

**Should I diversify or specialize?**
Diversify for resilience: grow 10 to 15 crops and raise 2 to 3 animal types. Specialize only if you have a reliable market, like selling to a restaurant. For beginners, diversification spreads risk.

## The bottom line

Farm management is not about perfection. It is about showing up daily, writing things down, and adjusting as you go. Start with a simple plan, set routines, track your money, and keep records. In your first year, you will make mistakes. That is normal. Learn from them, and each season gets smoother. Your farm does not have to be big to be well managed. It just has to be intentional.
