The best way to peel tomatoes for canning is to blanch them in boiling water for 30 to 60 seconds, then plunge them into an ice bath. This loosens the skin so it slips off with a paring knife or your fingers. It is fast, reliable, and works for any variety. For large batches, a tomato mill or food mill can skip peeling entirely, but blanching is the simplest and most cost-effective method for most home canners.

## Method 1: Blanching (the classic way)

This is the method most canners use. It works because the hot water expands the tomato's cells, separating the skin from the flesh.

### Step-by-step blanching

1. **Bring a large pot of water to a rolling boil.** Use a pot big enough to hold 3 to 4 tomatoes at a time without crowding.
2. **Prepare an ice bath.** Fill a large bowl with cold water and add a tray of ice cubes. You will need enough to cool the tomatoes quickly.
3. **Score the tomatoes.** Use a sharp paring knife to cut a shallow X on the bottom (the blossom end) of each tomato. This gives the skin a place to split and makes peeling easier.
4. **Blanch in batches.** Drop 3 to 4 tomatoes into the boiling water. Leave them for 30 to 60 seconds. The skin will start to split at the X. Do not overdo it, or the tomatoes will turn mushy.
5. **Transfer to the ice bath.** Use a slotted spoon to move the tomatoes to the ice water. Let them sit for 1 to 2 minutes, until they are cool enough to handle.
6. **Peel.** Lift a tomato out, and the skin should slide off easily. If it sticks, use the paring knife to gently pull it away. The flesh should be firm.
7. **Core and proceed.** Cut out the stem core and any green parts, then use the tomatoes as your recipe requires.

**Time:** About 10 minutes per 5 pounds of tomatoes, once the water is boiling.

**Cost:** No special equipment needed. A large pot and a slotted spoon are all you need.

## Method 2: Freezing (for a different texture)

Freezing tomatoes before peeling is another option, especially if you have freezer space. It works because ice crystals break down the cell walls, making the skin slip off easily after thawing.

### How to freeze-peel

1. **Wash and core the tomatoes.** Remove the stem core but leave the skin on.
2. **Place them in a single layer on a baking sheet.** Freeze for 2 to 3 hours, until solid.
3. **Transfer to freezer bags or containers.** You can keep them frozen for up to 6 months.
4. **Thaw when needed.** Put the frozen tomatoes in a bowl and let them thaw in the refrigerator or at room temperature. As they thaw, the skin will wrinkle and loosen.
5. **Peel.** Once thawed, the skin comes off easily with your fingers. The flesh will be softer and more watery, so this method is best for sauces or soups where you will cook them down anyway.

**Time:** Freezing takes a few hours, but active work is minimal.

**Cost:** Freezer bags or containers, about $5 to $10 for a pack of 20.

**Note:** Frozen tomatoes will have a softer texture, so they are not ideal for canning whole or in chunks. Use them for purees and sauces.

## Method 3: Using a food mill (no peeling needed)

If you are making sauce or juice, you can skip peeling entirely. A food mill separates the skin and seeds from the pulp as you crank or process the tomatoes.

### How to use a food mill

1. **Quarter the tomatoes** (no need to peel or core).
2. **Cook them down** in a large pot over medium heat for 10 to 15 minutes, until soft.
3. **Run through the food mill.** Place the mill over a bowl, add a batch of cooked tomatoes, and crank. The pulp will pass through, while skins and seeds stay behind.

**Time:** About 15 minutes per 5 pounds, including cooking.

**Cost:** A basic food mill costs $25 to $40. A motorized tomato strainer (like a Squeezo) runs $100 to $150 but speeds up large batches.

**Best for:** Sauces, tomato juice, and purees. Not for canning whole or diced tomatoes.

## Which method is best for canning?

For canning whole or diced tomatoes, blanching is the winner. It keeps the flesh firm and the texture intact, which is what you want for a jar of whole tomatoes. For sauces, a food mill saves time and gives you a smooth product without any peeling effort. Freezing is a good option if you are short on time during peak harvest and want to process later.

## Tips for easier peeling

- **Use ripe but firm tomatoes.** Overripe tomatoes get mushy and are harder to peel cleanly.
- **Work in small batches.** Do not overcrowd the boiling water, or the temperature drops and the skin won't loosen.
- **Use a timer.** 30 seconds is enough for small tomatoes, 60 seconds for large ones. Check one to see if the skin is splitting.
- **Peel over a bowl.** Catch any juice that drips, and add it to your canning pot.
- **Try a peeler for thin-skinned varieties.** Some heirlooms like Brandywine have delicate skins that come off with a regular vegetable peeler, no blanching needed.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Boiling too long.** Tomatoes will start to cook, and the flesh will turn soft and break apart.
- **Not cooling enough.** If the tomatoes are still hot, the skin will stick. Give them a full minute or two in the ice bath.
- **Skipping the score.** The X is essential for the skin to split evenly. Without it, you will have to wrestle with the skin.
- **Using a dull knife.** A sharp knife makes a clean cut and prevents crushing the tomato.

## FAQ

### Can I peel tomatoes without blanching?

Yes, you can use a serrated peeler or a regular vegetable peeler on firm, ripe tomatoes. It takes longer, but it works for small batches. Or use a food mill to skip peeling altogether.

### Do I need to peel tomatoes for salsa?

It depends on your preference. Peeling gives a smoother salsa; leaving the skin on adds texture and a bit of chew. For canning salsa, many recipes call for peeled tomatoes to ensure even heat penetration and a better texture.

### How long can I store peeled tomatoes before canning?

Peeled tomatoes should be processed within 1 to 2 hours. If you need to delay, keep them in the refrigerator for up to 24 hours, but no longer, to avoid spoilage.

### What is the fastest way to peel a large batch (50 pounds or more)?

For large batches, a motorized tomato strainer is the fastest. It processes 50 pounds in about 30 minutes, and you do not need to peel or core. If you prefer blanching, enlist a helper: one person blanches, another peels, and a third cores.

## The bottom line

Blanching is the best all-around method for peeling tomatoes for canning because it is fast, cheap, and preserves the tomato's texture. For sauces, a food mill is a worthwhile investment if you can a lot. Freezing is a convenient backup when time is tight. Whichever method you choose, start with ripe, firm tomatoes and work in small batches. Your canned goods will be smoother, tastier, and worth the effort.
