If you're in Tennessee and wondering which onions to grow, the answer depends on your zone: most of the state is USDA zones 6 and 7, so you need short-day or intermediate-day varieties. These types bulb properly with our day length and mild winters. For a reliable crop, plant short-day onions like 'Red Creole' or 'Texas SuperSweet' in the fall (October to November) or very early spring (February to March). Here's everything you need to know to get a good harvest.

## Understanding Day Length and Tennessee's Climate

Onions bulb based on day length, not just temperature. Short-day onions start forming bulbs when days reach 10 to 12 hours, which happens early in the season. Intermediate-day onions need 12 to 14 hours. In Tennessee, our latitude means short-day onions are the safest bet, but intermediates also work, especially in the northern part of the state.

Tennessee's climate is humid and hot in summer, with mild winters. This means you can plant onions in the fall for an early summer harvest, or in early spring for a later one. Fall planting often yields larger onions because the plants get established before winter. Spring planting works too, but you'll get smaller bulbs.

## Best Onion Varieties for Tennessee

Here are proven varieties that perform well in Tennessee's zones 6 and 7:

| Variety | Type | Color | Days to Maturity | Notes |

| 'Red Creole' | Short-day | Red | 95-110 | Sweet, stores well, great for southern gardens |
| 'Texas SuperSweet' | Short-day | Yellow | 100-115 | Very sweet, mild, best eaten fresh |

For storage, choose 'Red Creole' or 'Candy'. For fresh eating, 'Texas SuperSweet' and 'White Bermuda' are excellent. If you want a mix, plant a few of each.

## When and How to Plant

### Fall Planting (Recommended)

Plant sets or transplants from October 1 to November 15 in zones 6 and 7. This gives onions time to grow roots before winter dormancy. In zone 6, aim for early October; in zone 7, you can wait until November.

### Spring Planting

If you missed fall planting, plant as soon as the soil can be worked, usually late February to early March. Onions can tolerate light frost, so don't wait too long.

### Steps for Planting

1. Choose a sunny spot with well-drained soil. Onions need at least 6 hours of sun.
2. Work in 2 inches of compost or aged manure. Onions are heavy feeders.
3. Plant sets or transplants 1 inch deep, with the pointy end up. Space them 4 to 6 inches apart in rows 12 to 18 inches apart.
4. Water thoroughly after planting. Keep the soil consistently moist but not soggy.
5. Mulch with straw or grass clippings to retain moisture and suppress weeds.

## Caring for Onions Through the Season

### Watering

Onions need about 1 inch of water per week. In Tennessee's hot summers, you may need to water more often. Use a soaker hose or drip irrigation to keep foliage dry and prevent disease.

### Fertilizing

Fertilize every 3 weeks with a balanced fertilizer like 10-10-10. Stop fertilizing once the bulbs start to swell (usually late spring). Too much nitrogen can cause lush tops but small bulbs.

### Weeding

Weeds compete with onions for nutrients and water. Hand-pull or hoe carefully, as onion roots are shallow.

### Pest and Disease Management

Watch for onion thrips (small, pale insects) and fungal diseases like purple blotch. Use insecticidal soap for thrips and rotate crops to prevent disease. Avoid overhead watering in the evening.

## Harvesting and Storing

Onions are ready to harvest when the tops flop over and turn yellow. This usually happens in June for fall-planted onions, and July for spring-planted ones.

1. Gently pull or dig up the onions on a dry day.
2. Leave them in the field for a day or two to cure, if the weather is dry. Otherwise, cure in a well-ventilated area out of direct sun.
3. Once the tops are completely dry, cut them off, leaving 1 inch of stem.
4. Store in a cool, dry, dark place with good air circulation. Use mesh bags or hang them in braids.

Short-day onions typically store for 2 to 4 months. 'Red Creole' and 'Candy' store longer than sweet varieties like 'Texas SuperSweet'.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- Planting long-day onions: These won't bulb properly in Tennessee. Stick to short-day or intermediate.
- Planting too deep: Onions need to sit just below the soil surface. Planting too deep can cause rot.
- Overwatering: Onions don't like wet feet. Ensure good drainage.
- Skipping fall planting: Fall-planted onions often produce larger bulbs. Don't miss this window.

## FAQ

### Can I grow onions from seed in Tennessee?

Yes, but it takes longer. Start seeds indoors 8 to 10 weeks before your last frost date, then transplant. Sets and transplants are easier for beginners and give quicker results.

### What's the best onion for storage in Tennessee?

'Red Creole' is a top choice for storage, keeping for several months. 'Candy' also stores well, though it's an intermediate type.

### When should I harvest onions in Tennessee?

For fall-planted onions, harvest in June. For spring-planted, harvest in July. Look for tops that have flopped over and started to yellow.

### Can I grow sweet onions like Vidalia in Tennessee?

You can grow similar varieties like 'Texas SuperSweet' or 'Candy', but true Vidalia onions are specific to Georgia's soil and climate. Our Tennessee soil will produce a slightly different flavor, but still sweet and mild.

## The bottom line

For Tennessee gardeners, short-day onions are the way to go. Plant 'Red Creole', 'Texas SuperSweet', or 'Candy' in the fall for the best results. Give them full sun, consistent moisture, and regular feeding, and you'll have a solid harvest by early summer. Avoid long-day varieties, and don't forget to cure your onions properly for storage. With these tips, you'll be pulling sweet, homegrown onions in no time.
