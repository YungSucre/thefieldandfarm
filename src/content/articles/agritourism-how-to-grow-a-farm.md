Starting a farm is not about buying land and planting seeds. It is about making a plan, testing your soil, and starting small. This guide walks you through the first year of building a small farm, with realistic numbers and steps you can take this week.

## Assess your land and resources

Before you buy anything, evaluate what you have. If you already own land, get a soil test from your local extension office. It costs $15 to $30 and tells you pH, nutrient levels, and organic matter. If you are leasing, look for at least 1 acre of open, well-drained land with access to water. For a small market farm, 1 to 5 acres is enough to start.

Check your water source. You need at least 1,000 gallons per day for a 1-acre vegetable operation in peak summer. If you rely on rainfall, plan for drip irrigation and mulch to conserve moisture.

## Choose your farm type

You cannot grow everything at once. Pick one or two enterprises that match your land, climate, and market. For beginners, vegetables, cut flowers, or pastured poultry are the most manageable.

| Farm type | Startup cost (2026) | Time to first income | Skill level |
|-----------|---------------------|----------------------|-------------|
| Vegetables (1 acre) | $10,000-$20,000 | 3-4 months | Moderate |
| Cut flowers (1/2 acre) | $5,000-$10,000 | 2-3 months | Low |
| Pastured broilers (100 birds) | $1,500-$2,500 | 8-10 weeks | Low |
| Goat dairy (5 goats) | $5,000-$8,000 | 6-12 months | High |

Start with one crop or animal type. For example, grow 50 tomato plants and 100 cut flower stems, not 20 different vegetables.

## Plan your first season

Use your USDA hardiness zone to set planting dates. For a spring garden in zones 5-7, start cool-season crops like lettuce, spinach, and peas in March or April. Warm-season crops like tomatoes, peppers, and squash go out after the last frost, usually mid-May.

Make a simple crop plan: list what you will grow, how many feet of row, and expected yield. For a 100-foot row of tomatoes, you can expect 100 to 150 pounds of fruit. For a 100-foot row of carrots, 80 to 120 pounds.

## Get the right tools

You do not need a tractor. For the first year, a good set of hand tools is enough. Buy a stainless steel digging fork ($40-$60), a hoe ($25-$40), a hand trowel ($15-$25), and a wheel hoe ($150-$250) if you have more than 1,000 square feet. A walk-behind tiller ($500-$1,000) helps for initial bed preparation, but avoid over-tilling.

For irrigation, invest in drip tape and a filter. A basic drip kit for 100 feet costs $30-$50. A 50-gallon rain barrel ($100-$150) can supplement water for a small plot.

## Start seeds and transplants

If you have a sunny windowsill or a small greenhouse, start seeds 6-8 weeks before your last frost. Use a seed-starting mix ($10-$15 per bag) and a heat mat ($30-$50) for warm-season crops. For beginners, buy transplants from a local nursery for tomatoes, peppers, and cabbage. They cost $3-$5 per plant, but save you weeks of fussing.

Direct sow easy crops: beans, corn, squash, radishes, and carrots. Follow the seed packet for depth and spacing. For example, plant bean seeds 1 inch deep and 2 inches apart, in rows 18 inches apart.

## Manage weeds, pests, and water

Weeds are your biggest enemy. Mulch with straw or compost around plants to suppress weeds and retain moisture. A 2-3 inch layer of organic mulch costs $20-$40 per cubic yard. Hand-pull weeds weekly, especially after rain.

For pests, use row cover (floating fabric) to protect young plants from insects. It costs $0.50-$1 per linear foot. For larger pests like deer, install a 6-foot fence ($2-$4 per foot).

Water deeply and less often: 1 inch per week is a good rule. Use a rain gauge to track rainfall.

## Harvest and sell

Harvest in the morning, when vegetables are crisp. For cut flowers, pick in the cool of the day and put stems in water immediately.

Sell at a farmers market, a roadside stand, or through a CSA (community supported agriculture). A 20-share CSA for vegetables can bring in $4,000-$6,000 per season. A farmers market booth costs $20-$50 per day. Start with one market or 10 CSA shares.

## Scale up gradually

Do not double your acreage every year. Add one new crop or one new market each season. Track your expenses and income in a simple spreadsheet. In year one, expect to break even at best. By year three, a well-run 1-acre vegetable farm can net $20,000-$40,000, but that is after you have learned the ropes.

## FAQ

**How much money do I need to start a small farm?**
For a 1-acre vegetable farm, budget $10,000-$20,000 for tools, seeds, soil amendments, and irrigation. If you already have land and basic tools, you can start with $2,000-$3,000.

**Can I farm part-time while working a job?**
Yes, but start small. A 1/4-acre vegetable plot or 50 broiler chickens can be managed with 10-15 hours per week. Choose crops that do not need daily harvesting, like winter squash or potatoes.

**What is the easiest crop for a beginner?**
Radishes, lettuce, and green beans are the easiest. They germinate quickly, have few pests, and give you a harvest within 30-60 days. For a first-year market, try cut flowers like zinnias and sunflowers, which are low-maintenance and sell well.

**Do I need a tractor?**
No. For the first year, hand tools and a walk-behind tiller are enough. A tractor is a major expense and often unnecessary for small plots. Rent one if you need to break new ground.

## The bottom line

Growing a farm is a process, not a single event. Start with a soil test, pick one crop, and plant a small area. Use hand tools, water wisely, and sell what you grow. Expect a learning curve, but with careful planning and steady work, you can turn a patch of land into a productive farm. Begin this week by testing your soil and writing a one-page crop plan.
