The best method for canning tomatoes is hot pack processing in a boiling water bath, using a tested recipe with added acid (bottled lemon juice or citric acid). This method is safe, simple, and yields a better texture and flavor than raw pack. For those who prefer a more rustic, chunkier product, raw pack works too, but hot pack is more reliable for even heating and less headspace adjustment. Below, I break down the exact steps, tools, and varieties that work best, plus the numbers you need for 2026.

## Why hot pack beats raw pack

Hot packing means you heat the tomatoes to boiling before filling jars. This removes air, shrinks the tomatoes, and allows you to pack more into each jar. It also reduces the risk of spoilage because the food is already hot when it goes into the jar, so processing time is more effective. Raw pack, where you stuff raw tomatoes into jars and add hot liquid, is faster but can result in floating tomatoes, more headspace, and a less consistent product. For beginners, hot pack is

 the best method because it is forgiving and produces a shelf-stable product that tastes like summer.

## Equipment you need (and what it costs)

You likely already have a large stockpot, but for water bath canning you need a dedicated canner with a rack. A basic water bath canner with a jar rack runs $20 to $40 at hardware stores or online. You also need:

- Canning jars: quart or pint, with new lids and bands. A case of 12 quart jars costs about $15 to $20; lids are $3 to $5 per dozen.
- Jar lifter: $8 to $12.
- Bubble remover and headspace tool: $5 to $10.
- Kitchen scale (optional but helpful): $15 to $25.
- Large pot for blanching: you can use your stockpot.

If you want to can tomatoes without added acid, you must use a pressure canner. A dial-gauge pressure canner costs $100 to $300. But for most home canners, water bath with acid is the best method because it is cheaper, faster, and perfectly safe when you follow the recipe.

## Step-by-step: hot pack canning

1. **Select and wash tomatoes.** Use firm, ripe, disease-free tomatoes. Avoid overripe or bruised ones. Wash them well under running water.
2. **Blanch and peel.** Score an X on the bottom of each tomato. Drop into boiling water for 30 to 60 seconds, then transfer to an ice bath. The skins slip off easily. Remove cores and any green parts.
3. **Chop or crush.** For crushed tomatoes, cut into chunks and crush with a potato masher or spoon. For whole or halved, keep them as is.
4. **Heat the tomatoes.** Put the prepared tomatoes in a large pot and bring to a boil, stirring frequently to prevent sticking. Boil for 5 minutes.
5. **Add acid to each jar.** For pints, add 1 tablespoon bottled lemon juice or 1/4 teaspoon citric acid. For quarts, use 2 tablespoons lemon juice or 1/2 teaspoon citric acid. This is non-negotiable for water bath canning.
6. **Fill jars.** Using a ladle and funnel, pack hot tomatoes into hot jars, leaving 1/2 inch headspace. Remove air bubbles with the bubble tool.
7. **Wipe rims, place lids, and screw bands.** Wipe jar rims with a clean damp cloth. Center the lid and screw the band until fingertip tight.
8. **Process in water bath.** Place jars on the rack in the canner with simmering water (180°F). Add boiling water to cover jars by 1 to 2 inches. Bring to a rolling boil, then start timing.
9. **Process times.** For pints, process 35 minutes at altitudes up to 1,000 feet. For quarts, process 45 minutes. Add 5 minutes for every additional 1,000 feet of altitude above sea level. Adjust for your elevation.
10. **Cool and store.** Turn off heat, remove lid, and let jars sit in water for 5 minutes. Then lift out and cool on a towel for 12 to 24 hours. Check seals: lids should be concave and not pop when pressed. Store in a cool, dark place.

## Raw pack alternative

If you prefer raw pack, fill jars with raw peeled tomatoes, pressing to release juice, and add no water. Add acid as above. Leave 1/2 inch headspace. Process pints for 40 minutes, quarts for 50 minutes (adjust for altitude). Raw pack tomatoes will float, and the liquid level may drop during processing, but that is normal. The texture is softer, so I recommend hot pack for better quality.

## Tomato varieties that can well

Not all tomatoes are equal for canning. Paste tomatoes have less water and more flesh, making them ideal for sauce and canning. Good varieties include:

- **Roma**: classic, heavy yielder, thick walls.
- **San Marzano**: sweeter, meaty, great flavor.
- **Amish Paste**: large, meaty, heirloom.
- **Ropreco**: a paste type with good disease resistance.

For a more flavorful, juicy canned tomato, you can use slicing types like **Brandywine** or **Cherokee Purple**, but they will be more watery. If you use them, you may need to simmer longer to reduce liquid.

## Cost and yield in 2026

Growing your own tomatoes: a packet of seeds costs $3 to $6, and a plant yields 10 to 20 pounds per season. If you buy at a farmers market, expect to pay $1.50 to $3 per pound for canning tomatoes in season. A bushel (about 53 pounds) yields roughly 15 to 20 quarts of canned tomatoes, depending on variety and how much you cook down. So a quart of home-canned tomatoes costs about $1 to $2 in ingredients, plus your time and energy.

## Safety notes

- Always use a tested recipe from a reliable source like the USDA or your county extension.
- Do not reduce the amount of acid; it is essential for safety.
- Check your altitude and adjust processing time.
- If you have any doubt about spoilage (bulging lids, off odors, mold), discard the jar without tasting.

## FAQ

**Can I can tomatoes without adding acid?**
Yes, but only if you use a pressure canner. The USDA recommends pressure canning for tomatoes without acid. For water bath, acid is required to prevent botulism.

**How long do canned tomatoes last?**
Properly canned tomatoes will keep for 12 to 18 months, but they are best within a year. After that, quality declines.

**Can I use vinegar instead of lemon juice?**
Yes, you can use 4 tablespoons of 5% acidity vinegar per quart, but it may affect flavor. Lemon juice or citric acid is preferred.

**Why did my tomatoes float?**
Floating is common, especially with raw pack. It does not affect safety. Hot pack reduces floating.

## The bottom line

Hot pack water bath canning with added acid is the best method for most home canners. It is safe, simple, and yields a quality product. Invest in a water bath canner and good jars, use paste tomatoes, and follow the times exactly. You will have shelf-stable tomatoes that taste far better than store-bought, and you will have done it yourself. Start with a small batch, and you will be canning every summer from then on.
