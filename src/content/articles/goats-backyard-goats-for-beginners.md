If you're thinking about getting backyard goats, start with two things: sturdy fencing and a clear plan for hoof trimming. Goats are smart, social, and surprisingly destructive if given the chance. With the right setup, they'll clear brush, provide milk, and make you laugh daily. Here's what you need to know before you bring them home.

![Backyard goats for beginners](/images/articles/backyard-goats-for-beginners/section-1.jpg)

## Choose the right breed

Not all goats are equal for a backyard. For milk, consider Nigerian Dwarf or Miniature Nubian: they produce about 1 to 2 quarts per day, and their smaller size makes them easier to handle. For meat or brush control, Boer or Kiko crosses are hardy and efficient. If you want a pet that's easy to manage, Pygmy goats are friendly but can be stubborn.

For beginners, I recommend Nigerian Dwarfs. They're small (17 to 21 inches at the shoulder), friendly, and their milk is high in butterfat,

 great for soap and cheese. They also eat less than full-sized goats, which keeps costs down.

## Fencing: the non-negotiable

Goats are escape artists. You need fencing that's at least 4 feet high for Nigerian Dwarfs, 5 feet for larger breeds. Use woven wire (no-climb) with 2x4 inch openings, not field fence with larger gaps. Set posts every 8 feet, and bury the bottom 6 inches or add a hot wire at nose height. A solar charger costs $100 to $200, and the wire is about $0.50 per foot. Plan on $300 to $600 for a small pen (100 square feet per goat).

I learned the hard way: my first goat climbed a 4-foot fence and ate my neighbor's roses. Don't skip the hot wire.

## Housing and space

Goats need shelter from rain, wind, and extreme sun. A three-sided shed works, with 10 square feet per goat. Bedding: straw or wood shavings, about $10 per bale, changed weekly. They also need a dry area to lie down; wet hooves lead to rot.

Space: at least 200 square feet per goat for exercise, plus a pasture or browse area. If you have a quarter acre, you can keep two or three goats. They'll eat brush, but they also need hay and grain.

## Feeding: hay, grain, minerals

Goats are browsers, not grazers. They prefer leaves, twigs, and weeds over grass. Feed good quality grass hay (timothy or orchard grass) free choice. A goat eats about 2 to 4 pounds of hay per day, costing $10 to $20 per month per goat. Grain (12% protein) is only for milking does or growing kids, about 1 pound per day, $15 per 50-pound bag. Always provide loose goat minerals with copper, about $15 per 10-pound tub, and fresh water daily.

Avoid alfalfa for adult males; it can cause urinary stones. And never feed moldy hay.

## Daily care and health

Goats need daily attention: check for signs of illness, clean water, and feed. Hoof trimming every 4 to 8 weeks is essential. You'll need hoof trimmers ($15 to $30) and a bit of practice. Learn from a vet or experienced goat owner; untrimmed hooves cause lameness.

Common health issues: parasites (worms), especially in humid areas. Use fecal testing and deworm only when needed. Vaccinate for enterotoxemia and tetanus (CD&T) annually, about $5 per dose. Keep a basic first aid kit: iodine, wound spray, and electrolytes.

## Costs in 2026

Here's a realistic budget for two Nigerian Dwarf goats:

- Goats: $200 to $400 each (registered kids from a good breeder)
- Fencing and shelter: $500 to $1,000 (if you build yourself)
- Annual feed and hay: $400 to $600
- Vet and hoof care: $100 to $200 per year
- Misc (minerals, trimmers, etc.): $100

Total first year: about $1,300 to $2,300. After that, $600 to $900 per year.

## Getting started this week

1. Check local zoning: some areas limit livestock. Call your county office.
2. Visit a goat farm: see how they handle goats, ask about breeds.
3. Build or repair fencing: start with a small pen, then add pasture.
4. Order hay and minerals: find a local feed store.
5. Find a vet: ask for one who treats goats, not just cats and dogs.

## FAQ

**Q: How many goats should I start with?**
A: At least two. Goats are herd animals and get lonely, which leads to stress and health problems. Two are easier than one.

**Q: Do goats need a companion if I only want milk?**
A: Yes, but you can have a wether (neutered male) as a companion for a milking doe. That way you get milk and the goat has company.

**Q: Can I keep a goat in my backyard if I have a small lot?**
A: Yes, if you have at least 200 square feet per goat and good fencing. Nigerian Dwarfs are ideal for small spaces.

**Q: How much time do goats need daily?**
A: Plan on 30 minutes a day for feeding, checking, and cleaning. Add time for hoof trimming every month or two.

## The bottom line

Backyard goats are rewarding but not a low-maintenance pet. They need good fencing, daily care, and a financial commitment. Start with two Nigerian Dwarfs, invest in solid fencing, and learn hoof trimming early. With that foundation, you'll enjoy fresh milk, brush control, and a lot of personality. If you're not ready for that, consider volunteering at a farm or taking a goat care class first. It's better to be prepared than to learn the hard way like I did.
