If you are deciding between amaranth and barley, the short answer is: grow barley if you want a reliable, cool-season grain for flour or animal feed, and grow amaranth if you have hot summers, poor soil, or want a gluten-free pseudo-grain with more protein. Both are worth a spot, but they serve different purposes and need different timing. Here is what I have learned from growing both in my own fields and from talking to neighbors who do the same.

## Climate and growing season

Barley is a cool-season crop. It thrives in USDA zones 3 to 8 and needs about 60 to 90 days from planting to harvest, depending on variety. Plant it as soon as the soil can be worked in early spring, usually 2 to 4 weeks before your last frost date. In mild winter areas (zones 7 and warmer), you can also plant it in fall for a spring harvest. Barley does not like heat: once temperatures regularly hit 85°F, it stops filling grain and yields drop.

Amaranth is a warm-season crop. It needs soil temperatures above 65°F to germinate, so wait until after your last frost. It grows best in zones 4 to 10, but it really shines in hot, dry conditions where barley would fail. ## Yield and space

Yields vary widely, but here are realistic numbers from my experience and from university trials:

- Barley: 1 to 2 pounds of grain per 100 square feet, or about 20 to 40 bushels per acre (48 pounds per bushel). In a home garden, a 10x10 foot bed gives you roughly 1.5 pounds of hulled barley.
- Amaranth: 1 to 1.5 pounds of grain per 100 square feet, or about 1,000 to 2,000 pounds per acre (which is 20 to 40 bushels at 50 pounds per bushel). A 10x10 foot bed yields about 1 pound of amaranth grain.

Both are space-efficient for home use, but barley gives more grain per square foot in cool climates, while amaranth outyields barley in hot, dry conditions.

## Planting and care

### Barley

- Spacing: sow seeds 1 inch deep, 1 to 2 inches apart in rows 6 to 8 inches apart. For a dense stand, broadcast and rake in.
- Soil: prefers well-drained loam with pH 6.0 to 7.5. It tolerates poor soil but yields drop.
- Water: needs about 1 inch per week during tillering and heading. Too much water can cause lodging.
- Fertilizer: moderate nitrogen, about 50 to 80 pounds per acre. Too much nitrogen makes the straw weak.

### Amaranth

- Spacing: sow seeds 1/4 inch deep, 12 to 18 inches apart in rows 18 to 24 inches apart. Thin to 12 inches for larger heads.
- Soil: tolerant of poor, dry soil, but prefers well-drained loam with pH 6.0 to 7.0.
- Water: drought-tolerant once established, but water during flowering and grain fill for better yields.
- Fertilizer: light feeder, 20 to 40 pounds of nitrogen per acre. Too much makes it leafy and delays flowering.

## Harvest and processing

### Barley

Harvest when the straw turns yellow and the grain is hard, usually 60 to 90 days after planting. Cut the stalks, bundle them, and let them dry for a week or two. Thresh by beating the bundles or using a grain flail. Then winnow to remove chaff. The grain still has a hull that is tough to remove. For home use, you can store it as hulled barley (the hull stays on) or try to dehull with a barley pearler, which costs $200 to $400. Most home growers just use it as feed or sprout it. For flour, you need to remove the hull, which is labor-intensive without a mill.

### Amaranth

Harvest when the flower heads are dry and the seeds fall easily when rubbed, usually 90 to 120 days after planting. Cut the heads and place them in a paper bag to finish drying. Thresh by rubbing the heads over a screen or using a bucket and a stick. Winnow to remove chaff. Amaranth seeds are tiny, so use a fine screen (1/16 inch) and a gentle breeze. No hull to remove, so it is ready to cook or grind right away.

## Nutrition and uses

Barley is a true cereal grain with gluten. It is high in fiber, especially beta-glucan, which helps lower cholesterol. Use it in soups, stews, or as a rice substitute. You can also malt it for beer. Amaranth is a pseudo-grain, gluten-free, and higher in protein (about 14%) and lysine, an amino acid often lacking in grains. Use it as a hot cereal, pop it like popcorn, or grind it into flour for baking. Amaranth flour has no gluten, so mix it with wheat flour for bread.

## Cost and availability

In 2026, seed prices are:

- Barley seed: $10 to $20 per pound, enough for 500 to 1,000 square feet.
- Amaranth seed: $5 to $10 per ounce, enough for 200 to 400 square feet (seeds are tiny).

Grain prices at retail: hulled barley $1.50 to $2.50 per pound, amaranth grain $4 to $6 per pound. Growing your own saves money only if you value the experience, because labor and processing time are significant.

## Which should you choose?

Choose barley if:
- You have a cool spring and want a reliable grain for flour or animal feed.
- You are willing to deal with hulling or have a pearler.
- You want a traditional cereal grain for soups and brewing.

Choose amaranth if:
- You have hot summers or poor, dry soil.
- You need a gluten-free grain with high protein.
- You want an easy-to-process grain with no hull.

Both are rewarding, but they are not interchangeable. I grow both: barley in the spring, then amaranth in the same bed after barley harvest, which gives me two grains from one plot.

## FAQ

### Can I grow amaranth in a cold climate?

Yes, but choose a short-season variety like 'Golden Giant' (90 days) and start seeds indoors 4 to 6 weeks before the last frost. In zone 4, you might get a harvest if you have a warm fall, but it is risky. Barley is a safer bet in cold climates.

### Do I need special equipment to process barley?

For small amounts, you can thresh and winnow by hand, but removing the hull is the hard part. A barley pearler costs $200 to $400. If you do not want to invest, use barley as animal feed or sprout it. Amaranth is much easier: no hull to remove.

### Which grain has more protein?

Amaranth has about 14% protein, while barley has about 12%. Amaranth also has more lysine, making it a complete protein. For gluten-free diets, amaranth is the clear choice.

### Can I plant both in the same season?

Yes, in many areas. Plant barley in early spring, harvest in early summer, then plant amaranth in the same bed for a fall harvest. This works in zones 5 to 8, where you have enough warm days left.

## The bottom line

Barley is a reliable cool-season grain with gluten, but it requires hulling. Amaranth is a heat-loving, gluten-free pseudo-grain that is easier to process. Choose based on your climate and how you plan to use the grain. Start small, maybe a 10x10 foot bed, and see which one fits your rotation. Both are worth growing, and you can even grow both in one season if you time it right.
