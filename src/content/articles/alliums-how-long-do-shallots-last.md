Shallots last 6 to 8 months in a cool, dark, dry place if cured properly, but only 2 to 4 weeks in the fridge and up to 12 months in the freezer. The key is curing: without it, even the best storage spot won't save them. Here's exactly how to get the longest life out of your harvest, plus what to do when you have more than you can use.

## Why curing matters

Shallots are perishable because they hold moisture in their necks and skins. Curing dries those layers, sealing out rot and mold. Skip curing and you'll see sprouting or soft spots within weeks. Plan for 2 to 3 weeks of curing after harvest, depending on your climate.

## Harvest and cure for maximum shelf life

Harvest shallots when the tops fall over and start to brown, usually 90 to 120 days after planting. In most zones, that's mid to late summer. Use a garden fork to lift them gently, then leave them in the field for a day if the weather is dry. If rain threatens, move them to a greenhouse, garage, or covered porch.

Cure in a single layer on a wire rack, old window screen, or wooden pallet. Aim for 75 to 85°F and 60 to 70% humidity. Good airflow is critical: a fan helps if the space is still. After 2 weeks, test by pressing the neck: it should feel dry and papery. If not, give them another week.

Once cured, trim the roots to 1/4 inch and cut the tops to 1 inch. Do not wash them; brush off loose dirt. Sort out any with cuts, soft spots, or thick necks. Use those first, within 2 to 4 weeks.

## Storage conditions that extend shelf life

The ideal storage spot is 32 to 40°F with 60 to 70% humidity, but most homes don't have that. A cool, dark pantry or basement at 50 to 60°F works well. Avoid the fridge: the moisture makes them sprout faster. Avoid sunlight, which triggers greening and sprouting.

Store in mesh bags, paper bags with holes, or loosely woven baskets. Do not use plastic bags; they trap moisture. Check every 2 weeks and remove any that are soft or sprouting. One bad shallot can spoil a whole batch.

Here's a quick reference for storage methods:

| Method | Temperature | Humidity | Shelf life |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cured, pantry | 50-60°F | 60-70% | 6-8 months |
| Fridge (uncured) | 35-40°F | High | 2-4 weeks |
| Freezer (chopped) | 0°F | N/A | Up to 12 months |
| Dehydrated | Room temp | Low | 12+ months |

## Freezing and dehydrating for longer storage

Freezing is the easiest way to keep shallots for a year. Peel and chop them, then spread in a single layer on a baking sheet. Freeze for 2 hours, then transfer to a freezer bag. They'll stay loose, so you can grab a handful. Use them straight from frozen in soups, stews, or sautés. They lose some crunch but keep full flavor.

Dehydrating is another option. Slice thinly and dry at 125°F for 6 to 8 hours until brittle. Grind into powder or store whole slices in an airtight jar. Rehydrate in warm water for 10 minutes before using. This method takes more time but uses less freezer space.

## How to tell if shallots have gone bad

Signs of spoilage: soft spots, dark mold, a sour or fermented smell, or green sprouts. A few sprouts are okay if you cut them off and use the shallot within a week, but they signal the end of storage life. If the flesh is mushy or slimy, toss it.

## Varieties that store best

Some shallots last longer than others. For long storage, choose:

- 'French Red' (also called 'Griselle'): classic, stores 6 to 8 months.
- 'Ambition': a Dutch variety with good disease resistance, stores 6 to 7 months.
- 'Conservor': a long-day hybrid, stores up to 8 months.

Avoid 'Prisma' or other soft-neck types if you need long storage; they're better for fresh eating.

## FAQ

**Can I store shallots in the fridge?**
Yes, but only for 2 to 4 weeks. The fridge's humidity causes sprouting. If you must use the fridge, keep them in a paper bag with holes and use them quickly.

**How do I know when shallots are fully cured?**
The necks feel dry and papery, and the skins rustle when you move them. Cut one open: the flesh should be firm and moist, not shriveled.

**Can I replant shallots that have sprouted?**
Yes, if they're still firm. Plant the whole bulb in early spring, and it will produce a new cluster. But for eating, use sprouted ones soon.

**Do shallots last longer than onions?**
Generally, yes. Shallots have a lower water content and thinner skins, so they store longer than most onions. Expect 6 to 8 months for shallots versus 3 to 6 months for standard onions.

## The bottom line

Cure your shallots properly for 2 to 3 weeks, store them in a cool, dark, dry place, and they'll last 6 to 8 months. Freeze or dehydrate extras for up to a year. Check them monthly and use any that show signs of spoilage first. With these steps, you'll have homegrown shallots from summer to spring.
