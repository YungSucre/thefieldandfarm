If you're growing onions in Pennsylvania, your best bet is to choose long-day varieties, as our latitude and summer day length trigger bulb formation. For most of the state (USDA zones 5-7), long-day onions are the reliable choice. Short-day onions will produce lots of tops but small bulbs, so skip them. Here's what to plant, when to plant, and how to get a good crop.

## Understanding Onion Day Length

Onions bulb based on day length. In Pennsylvania, we get about 14-16 hours of daylight in summer, which is perfect for long-day onions. These varieties start forming bulbs when days reach 14-16 hours. Short-day onions need 10-12 hours, so they bulb too early here and stay small. Intermediate-day onions can work in southern PA but are less reliable. Stick with long-day for consistent results.

For storage, go with 'Copra' or 'Red Zeppelin'. For fresh eating, 'Walla Walla' or 'Ailsa Craig' are excellent. If you want a mix, plant 50% storage, 25% sweet, 25% red.

## Planting Dates and Methods

In Pennsylvania, you can start from seed, sets, or transplants. Sets are the easiest for beginners, but transplants give the best yields. Here are the key dates:

- **Start seeds indoors:** 8-10 weeks before last frost, which is typically mid-April in zone 6. So start seeds in late February or early March.
- **Plant sets or transplants outdoors:** As soon as soil can be worked, usually late March to mid-April. Onions can handle light frost.
- **Direct sow seeds:** Not recommended in PA, as the season is too short for most varieties to mature.

Spacing: Plant sets or transplants 1 inch deep, 4 inches apart in rows 12-18 inches apart. For larger bulbs, space 6 inches apart. Onions need full sun and well-drained soil with pH 6.0-6.8.

## Soil Preparation and Fertilizing

Onions are heavy feeders. Before planting, work in 2-3 inches of compost or aged manure. Add a balanced fertilizer like 10-10-10 at 1 pound per 10 square feet. Side-dress with nitrogen (like blood meal) when bulbs start to swell, usually in late May. Keep the soil consistently moist, about 1 inch of water per week.

## Growing Tips for Pennsylvania

- **Mulch** with straw or grass clippings to retain moisture and suppress weeds.
- **Weed carefully**: Onions have shallow roots, so hand-pull weeds to avoid disturbing the bulbs.
- **Watch for pests**: Onion maggots and thrips can be issues. Use row covers early, and rotate crops.
- **Stop watering** when tops start to fall over, about 2-3 weeks before harvest.

## Harvesting and Curing

Harvest when tops have fallen over and started to brown, usually late July to early August. Gently lift bulbs with a fork, then cure in a warm, dry, airy place for 2-3 weeks. Cut tops to 1 inch and roots to 1/4 inch, then store in a cool, dry place. For storage varieties, keep at 32-40°F with low humidity.

## FAQ

**Can I grow short-day onions in Pennsylvania?**
Technically yes, but they'll bulb too early and stay small. Stick with long-day for best results.

**What's the best way to start onions: seeds, sets, or transplants?**
Sets are easiest for beginners, but transplants from seed give larger bulbs. If you start seeds, use a heat mat and grow lights.

**When should I harvest onions?**
When about half the tops have fallen over, start checking. Harvest all when most tops are down, and cure immediately.

**How do I store onions for winter?**
Cure thoroughly, then store in mesh bags or braided in a cool, dry, dark place with good air circulation. Check regularly for sprouting or rot.

## The bottom line

For Pennsylvania, plant long-day onion varieties like 'Copra', 'Red Zeppelin', and 'Walla Walla'. Start seeds indoors in late winter, or buy sets in early spring. Plant in well-drained soil with compost, keep them watered and weeded, and harvest in late summer. With a little care, you'll have a solid crop of onions for fresh eating and storage.
