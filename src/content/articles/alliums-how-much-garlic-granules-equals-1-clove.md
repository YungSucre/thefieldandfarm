If you're out of fresh garlic, use 1/2 teaspoon of garlic granules for every clove your recipe calls for. That's the standard ratio, but it's not perfect: granules are less pungent and have a slightly different flavor, so you may need to adjust to taste. Here's how to make the swap work in your kitchen.

## Why the Ratio Isn't Exact

Garlic granules are dehydrated and ground, which concentrates their flavor but also changes it. The heat of dehydration mellows some of the sharp, allicin-based bite you get from fresh garlic. So while 1/2 teaspoon matches the volume of one average clove (about 1 teaspoon minced), you might find the granule version tastes weaker. Start with 1/2 teaspoon, taste, and add a pinch more if needed. For a stronger, more fresh-like kick, you can rehydrate the granules in a little water or lemon juice for 5 minutes before adding them to your dish.

## Converting for Different Garlic Forms

Here's a quick reference for swapping garlic in recipes. These are averages based on a standard supermarket clove, which weighs about 3 to 7 grams.

| Fresh garlic form | Amount equivalent to 1 clove |
| --- | --- |
| Garlic granules | 1/2 teaspoon |
| Garlic powder | 1/8 teaspoon (it's more concentrated) |
| Garlic salt | 1/2 teaspoon (reduce added salt elsewhere) |
| Minced garlic (jarred) | 1/2 teaspoon |
| Garlic juice | 1/4 teaspoon |

Keep in mind that garlic salt is about 3 parts salt to 1 part garlic, so if you use it, cut back on other salt in the recipe.

## How to Measure Garlic Granules Accurately

Use a standard measuring spoon, not a kitchen spoon, which varies. Level the spoon with a knife or your finger. If you're doubling a recipe, measure twice rather than eyeballing. For large batches, a kitchen scale helps: 1 clove equals about 1.5 grams of granules. A 1-pound jar of granules (about $8 to $12) will last a long time, but for occasional use, a 3-ounce jar (around $4) is plenty.

## Adjusting for Your Dish

Granules work best in cooked dishes where they have time to rehydrate and meld, like soups, stews, marinades, and roasted vegetables. In raw applications, like salad dressings or aioli, granules can taste dusty. For those, rehydrate them first or use fresh garlic. Also, granules are more uniform, so they distribute evenly, which is nice for rubs and spice blends.

## Storing Garlic Granules

Keep granules in an airtight container in a cool, dark place. They'll last about 2 to 3 years, but check the best-by date. Heat and light degrade flavor faster. If they clump, that's moisture, and they may be past their prime.

## FAQ

### Can I use garlic granules in place of fresh in any recipe?

Yes, but expect a milder, slightly different flavor. For raw dishes, rehydrate them first. For cooked dishes, you can add them directly.

### How much garlic powder equals one clove?

Use 1/8 teaspoon of garlic powder for one clove. It's more concentrated than granules, so don't overdo it.

### Are garlic granules the same as garlic powder?

No. Granules are coarser and less dense, so they measure differently. Granules are about 1/2 teaspoon per clove, powder is 1/8 teaspoon.

### Can I substitute garlic granules in pickling or fermenting?

It's not recommended. Fresh garlic has enzymes and compounds that affect fermentation. Use fresh cloves for pickles or ferments.

## The Bottom Line

For most recipes, 1/2 teaspoon of garlic granules replaces one fresh clove. Taste and adjust from there. Keep a jar in your pantry for convenience, but for the best flavor, nothing beats fresh. Use granules when you're in a pinch or want a milder, more uniform garlic note.
