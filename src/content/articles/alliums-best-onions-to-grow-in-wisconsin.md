If you're growing onions in Wisconsin, your best bet is to choose long-day varieties, as our northern latitude (zones 3-5) gives them the 14-16 hours of daylight they need to bulb properly. Short-day onions will just grow tops and never form good bulbs. For reliable yields, plant sets or transplants in early spring, as soon as the soil can be worked (usually mid-April to early May). Here's what works, what doesn't, and how to get it right.

## Understanding Onion Day-Length Requirements

Onions bulb based on day length, not temperature. In Wisconsin, we get long summer days, so long-day onions are the only type that will reliably form bulbs. Short-day onions (like 'Texas SuperSweet') are for southern states and will produce only greens here. Intermediate-day onions (like 'Candy') can work in southern Wisconsin but are less reliable in the north.

Stick with long-day varieties for consistent results. They store well and have the classic pungent flavor that many gardeners prefer.

## Planting Dates and Spacing

In Wisconsin, plant onion sets or transplants as soon as the soil is workable in spring, typically mid-April in the south (zone 5) to early May in the north (zone 3). Soil temperature should be at least 40°F. If you start from seed, sow indoors 8-10 weeks before your last frost date (typically late March to early April) and transplant out after hardening off.

Spacing: Plant sets or transplants 1 inch deep, with 4 inches between plants and 12-18 inches between rows. For larger bulbs, space 6 inches apart. Onions are shallow-rooted, so keep the soil moist but not waterlogged.

## Soil Preparation and Fertilizer

Onions need well-drained, loose soil with a pH between 6.0 and 7.0. Work in 2-3 inches of compost before planting. Add a balanced fertilizer like 10-10-10 at planting time, about 1 cup per 10 feet of row. Side-dress with nitrogen (like blood meal) when plants are 6 inches tall, and again when bulbs start to swell.

Avoid fresh manure, as it can cause disease and poor bulb formation. Onions are heavy feeders, so don't skimp on fertility.

## Growing Tips for Wisconsin's Climate

Our cool springs and warm summers are ideal for onions, but watch for these issues:

- **Mulch** to retain moisture and suppress weeds. Use straw or grass clippings, applied 2-3 inches thick after planting.
- **Water** consistently: about 1 inch per week. Inconsistent moisture can cause splitting or poor bulb development.
- **Weed control** is critical, especially early. Onions don't compete well with weeds. Hand-pull or use a hoe carefully.
- **Pests**: Onion maggots can be a problem. Use row covers for the first few weeks, and rotate crops each year.
- **Diseases**: Downy mildew and purple blotch can occur in humid spells. Ensure good air circulation and avoid overhead watering in the evening.

## Harvesting and Curing

Harvest when tops start to yellow and fall over, usually late July to early August. Gently lift bulbs with a fork, then cure in a warm, dry, well-ventilated area for 2-3 weeks. After curing, cut tops to 1 inch and roots, then store in a cool, dry place (40-50°F) in mesh bags or crates.

Storage onions like 'Copra' can keep for 10 months if cured properly. Sweet onions like 'Walla Walla' are best used within 2-3 months.

## Cost and Where to Buy

For a 100-foot row, you'll need about 400 sets, costing $20-$30. You can buy sets online from companies like Dixondale Farms or Jung Seed, but local garden centers often have varieties suited to your area.

## FAQ

**Can I grow onions from seed in Wisconsin?** Yes, but start seeds indoors 8-10 weeks before last frost. It's more work, but you get more varieties and cheaper plants. Transplants are easier for beginners.

**What's the difference between sets and transplants?** Sets are small bulbs grown the previous year; they're easy but sometimes bolt (go to seed) if they're too large. Transplants are young plants started from seed; they give more control and often produce larger bulbs.

**Why are my onions not forming bulbs?** Likely reasons: wrong day-length type (short-day), planted too late, or too much nitrogen. Ensure you're growing long-day varieties and plant early.

**How do I store onions for winter?** Cure thoroughly, then store in a cool, dry place (40-50°F) with good air circulation. Check regularly and remove any that sprout or rot.

## The Bottom Line

For Wisconsin, choose long-day onion varieties like 'Copra', 'Patterson', and 'Red Wing'. Plant sets or transplants in early spring, give them fertile, well-drained soil, and consistent moisture. Harvest when tops fall over, cure properly, and you'll have onions that last through winter. Avoid short-day types, and don't delay planting. With a little care, you'll be pulling sweet, pungent bulbs by late summer.
