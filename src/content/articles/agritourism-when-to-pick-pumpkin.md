The short answer: pick your pumpkin when the rind is deep, uniform in color, the stem is brown and corky, and the skin resists a fingernail prick. This usually happens 90 to 120 days after planting, depending on the variety. For most of the US, that means late September to early October. But don't rely on the calendar alone: the plant gives you clear signals. Here's how to read them and get pumpkins that store for months.

## Signs your pumpkin is ready

A ripe pumpkin stops growing and starts hardening. Check these four signs before you cut:

- **Rind color**: The pumpkin has reached its full, deep color. For 'Howden' or 'Connecticut Field', that's a rich orange. For 'Jarrahdale', it's blue-gray; for 'Cinderella', it's a warm red-orange. If you see green streaks or patches, wait.
- **Stem**: The stem turns from green to brown and feels corky or woody. It should be dry, not pliable. A green stem means the pumpkin is still taking in nutrients.
- **Rind hardness**: Press your thumbnail into the skin. If it barely dents, the rind is hard. If it punctures easily, the pumpkin is not ready.
- **Sound**: A ripe pumpkin sounds hollow when you tap it. This is less reliable than the other signs, but it's a good backup.

## How to harvest without damaging the stem

Use sharp pruning shears or a knife, not your hands. Cut 3 to 4 inches of stem above the pumpkin. A longer stem helps prevent rot and gives you a handle. Never carry a pumpkin by the stem: it can break, and a broken stem shortens storage life.

After cutting, brush off dirt but do not wash. Washing can introduce moisture and mold. Set the pumpkin in the sun for a day or two to cure, if the weather is dry. Curing hardens the skin further and heals small cuts.

## Timing by zone and variety

Planting dates vary by your last frost date. Here's a rough guide for direct sowing or transplanting:

| USDA Zone | Last frost date | Plant pumpkins | Harvest window |
|-----------|-----------------|----------------|----------------|
| 3-4       | May 15 - June 1 | May 15 - June 1 | Late Sept - Oct |
| 5-6       | April 15 - May 1 | May 1 - May 15 | Late Sept - Oct |
| 7-8       | March 15 - April 1 | April 1 - May 1 | Sept - Oct |
| 9-10      | Feb 1 - March 1 | March 1 - April 1 | Aug - Sept |

Variety maturity matters. 'Sugar Pie' is ready in 100 days, 'Howden' in 110-120 days, and 'Atlantic Giant' takes 120-140 days. If you planted late, your pumpkins may need more time. In cool zones, you can cover plants with row covers to extend the season, but don't expect a full harvest if frost hits before maturity.

## What about green pumpkins?

If frost threatens and your pumpkins are still green, you have two options:

1. **Leave them on the vine until frost**: A light frost (32°F) can damage the rind, causing rot. If a hard freeze is coming, pick them immediately.
2. **Pick and ripen indoors**: Cut with a long stem, wash with a 10% bleach solution (1 part bleach to 9 parts water), dry, and place in a warm, dry spot (70-80°F) for 2-3 weeks. They won't turn fully orange, but they'll color up somewhat. They won't store as long, so use them first.

## Storing pumpkins after harvest

Cure pumpkins at 80-85°F with 80% humidity for 10-14 days if you can. This is ideal for long storage. If you don't have that setup, a warm, dry room works. After curing, store at 50-55°F with 50-70% humidity. A basement or cool closet works. Check monthly and remove any that soften or mold.

For eating pumpkins, you can use them right away or store them. 'Sugar Pie' and 'New England Pie' are the best for pies. 'Long Island Cheese' is another great eating variety. Carving pumpkins like 'Howden' are not as flavorful, but they store well.

## Tools and costs

You don't need much. A good pair of pruning shears costs $15-$25. A sharp knife works too. For curing, a wire rack or old screen helps air circulate. If you're buying pumpkins for a fall display, expect to pay $5-$15 each at a farm stand in 2026, depending on size.

## FAQ

**Can I pick a pumpkin before it's fully orange?**
Yes, if frost is coming. It will continue to color slightly indoors, but it won't be as sweet or store as long. Try to let it ripen on the vine for best quality.

**How do I know if a pumpkin is ripe for eating?**
The same signs apply: deep color, hard rind, brown stem. For eating, choose a variety like 'Sugar Pie' and harvest after the rind is hard. The flesh should be thick and deep orange.

**What if the stem breaks off?**
It's not the end of the world. Use that pumpkin first, as it will rot sooner. Keep it in a cool, dry place and check it often.

**Can I leave pumpkins on the vine after they're ripe?**
Yes, you can leave them for a few weeks, but watch for rot, especially if the ground is wet. Place a board or straw under the pumpkin to keep it dry.

## The bottom line

Pick pumpkins when the rind is hard, the color is deep, and the stem is brown. Cut with 3-4 inches of stem, cure, and store cool and dry. Timing is more about plant signals than the calendar, but for most of the US, late September to early October is the sweet spot. When in doubt, wait a week: a ripe pumpkin won't spoil on the vine, but an unripe one won't ripen well off it.
