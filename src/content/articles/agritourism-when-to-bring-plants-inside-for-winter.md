The short answer: bring plants inside when nighttime temperatures consistently drop below 50°F (10°C), which for most zones is 2 to 4 weeks before your average first frost date. For tender tropicals, that means acting before the thermometer hits 45°F, while hardier potted perennials can wait until just before a hard freeze. The key is to start the transition early, not when frost is already threatening.

## Know your zone and frost dates

Your first frost date is the trigger, but the exact move-in date depends on the plant's cold tolerance. Use the USDA Hardiness Zone map and your local extension service for precise dates. Here are typical first frost dates by zone (2026 averages):

| Zone | Average First Frost | Suggested Move-In Date |
|------|---------------------|------------------------|
| 3-4  | Sept 15-30          | Sept 1-15              |
| 5-6  | Oct 1-15            | Sept 15-Oct 1          |
| 7-8  | Oct 15-Nov 15       | Oct 1-15               |
| 9-10 | Nov 15-Dec 15       | Nov 1-15               |

If you don't know your zone, check the USDA map online or ask your local nursery. For example, a gardener in Zone 6 (like St. Louis) should plan to bring plants in by late September, while someone in Zone 9 (like Houston) can wait until November.

## Which plants need to come inside first

Not all plants need the same treatment. Prioritize based on cold sensitivity:

- **Tropicals (move in first):** Hibiscus, bougainvillea, mandevilla, citrus trees. These suffer below 50°F and die at 32°F. Move them when nights hit 50°F.
- **Semi-hardy (move in second):** Geraniums, coleus, fuchsias, begonias. They can handle 40°F briefly, but bring them in before frost.
- **Hardy potted perennials (can wait):** Hostas, daylilies, ornamental grasses. These can stay out until a hard freeze (28°F) and then go into a garage or unheated shed.

Check the plant's tag or do a quick search for its cold hardiness. When in doubt, err on the side of bringing it in early; a week inside is safer than a night of frost.

## How to transition plants indoors

Moving plants from outdoors to indoors is a shock. Do it gradually over 7 to 10 days:

1. **Inspect for pests:** Check leaves, stems, and soil for aphids, spider mites, or other insects. Spray with insecticidal soap (like Safer Brand, $8-$12 per 32 oz) if needed.
2. **Start with shade:** Move plants to a shaded porch or under a tree for 3-4 days. This helps them adjust to lower light.
3. **Bring in at night:** For the next 3-4 nights, bring them in at dusk and put them back out in the morning. This eases the transition.
4. **Final move:** Bring them inside to a bright spot, ideally a south-facing window or under a grow light.

During the transition, reduce watering slightly. Indoor air is drier, but plants use less water in lower light. Overwatering is the most common mistake; check the top inch of soil before watering.

## Prepare your indoor space

Before you bring plants in, set up the area. Most homes have low humidity (30-40% in winter), while tropicals prefer 50% or more. Group plants together to create a microclimate, or use a humidifier (a small one costs $20-$40). Place a tray of pebbles with water under pots to boost humidity.

Light is another challenge. Even a bright window may not provide enough for sun-loving plants. Consider a grow light, such as a GE BR30 LED bulb ($15-$25) or a full-spectrum panel ($40-$60). Position it 6-12 inches above the plants and run it for 12-14 hours a day.

Temperature matters too: keep indoor temps between 60-75°F. Avoid placing plants near drafts, heat vents, or fireplaces.

## What to do with plants that can't come inside

Not everything fits in your living room. For hardy perennials, you have options:

- **Garage or shed:** Move pots into an unheated garage or shed after the first hard frost. Water occasionally (monthly) to keep roots from drying out completely.
- **Bury pots:** Dig a trench in a garden bed and sink the pots up to their rims. Mulch with 4-6 inches of straw or leaves. This protects roots from freezing.
- **Create a cold frame:** Build or buy a simple cold frame (DIY kits $50-$100) to keep pots at a consistent temperature.

For tender bulbs like dahlias and cannas, dig them up, let them dry, and store in a cool, dark place (40-50°F) in peat moss or sawdust.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Waiting too long:** A sudden frost can kill tropicals in one night. Check the 10-day forecast regularly in fall.
- **Bringing in pests:** Inspect and treat before moving inside. A single aphid infestation can spread to all your houseplants.
- **Overwatering:** Indoor plants need less water. Let the soil dry slightly between waterings.
- **Skipping the transition:** Going straight from full sun to a dim corner causes leaf drop and stress.

## FAQ

**Q: Can I bring plants inside if they have already been exposed to frost?**
A: If the frost was light (above 30°F) and the plant looks undamaged, you can try. Move it to a cool, shaded spot for a few days, then bring it in. If leaves are black or mushy, cut off the damaged parts and hope for new growth. But if the stem is soft, the plant is likely done.

**Q: How do I know if my plant is too big to bring inside?**
A: If it's taller than 6 feet or wider than 3 feet, it may not fit. Consider taking cuttings instead: root 4-6 inch stem cuttings in water or moist potting mix. This gives you a smaller plant for winter and a new one for spring.

**Q: Should I fertilize before bringing plants in?**
A: No. Stop fertilizing about a month before moving indoors. Plants slow down in winter, and fertilizer can burn roots or encourage weak growth.

**Q: What if I don't have a sunny window?**
A: Use grow lights. A simple LED shop light with a warm white bulb works, or invest in a full-spectrum panel. Place it close (6-12 inches) and run it 12-14 hours daily. This is essential for flowering plants like citrus or hibiscus.

## The bottom line

Bring plants inside when nighttime lows hit 50°F, which is typically 2-4 weeks before your first frost date. Start with tropicals, then semi-hardy plants, and leave hardy perennials for later. Transition gradually over a week, inspect for pests, and set up a bright, humid spot. With a little planning, your potted plants will survive winter and thrive next spring. Check your local forecast weekly in September and October, and don't delay if cold nights are coming.
