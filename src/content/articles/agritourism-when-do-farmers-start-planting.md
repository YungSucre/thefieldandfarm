Farmers start planting at different times depending on their USDA hardiness zone, the crop, and the season. In most of the US, the first plantings of cold-hardy crops like peas and spinach go in the ground 2 to 4 weeks before the last frost date, while warm-season crops like tomatoes and corn wait until after the last frost. For example, in Zone 5, that means peas go in around late March or early April, and tomatoes go out in mid-May. But there is no single date: you need to know your zone and your crop's tolerance to cold.

## Know your last frost date

The single most important date for planting is your average last spring frost date. This is the date after which there is a 50% chance of no more frost. You can find it from the National Gardening Association or your local extension service. For reference, here are typical last frost dates by zone:

| Zone | Last frost date (approx.) |
|------|---------------------------|
| 3    | May 15 - June 1           |
| 4    | May 1 - May 15            |
| 5    | April 15 - May 1          |
| 6    | April 1 - April 15        |
| 7    | March 15 - April 1        |
| 8    | February 15 - March 15    |
| 9    | January 30 - February 15  |

These are averages, not guarantees. A late frost can happen up to two weeks after the average date. So many farmers use the "two-week rule": they wait two weeks after the last frost date to plant warm-season crops if they want to be extra safe, or they use row covers to protect early plantings.

## Cold-hardy crops: plant early

Cold-hardy crops can tolerate light frosts (down to 28°F) and even some freezing. They are the first to go in. In Zone 5, you can plant these 2 to 4 weeks before the last frost date, as soon as the soil can be worked in spring. That means late March to early April.

Common cold-hardy crops and their soil temperature needs:

- Peas: soil at least 40°F, plant 1 inch deep, 2 inches apart
- Spinach: soil at least 35°F, plant 1/2 inch deep, 3 inches apart
- Lettuce: soil at least 40°F, plant 1/4 inch deep, 6 inches apart
- Broccoli and cabbage: start indoors 6 weeks before last frost, transplant 2 weeks before last frost
- Onions: sets can go in 4 weeks before last frost, plant 1 inch deep, 4 inches apart

These crops can handle a light frost, but not a hard freeze. If a hard freeze is forecast, cover them with row cover or a cold frame.

## Warm-season crops: wait for soil to warm

Warm-season crops need both air and soil temperatures above a certain threshold. Soil temperature is key: tomatoes and peppers need soil at least 60°F, corn and beans need 65°F, and melons and cucumbers need 70°F. If you plant too early, seeds rot and transplants stunt.

In Zone 5, the last frost is around May 1, but soil may not reach 60°F until mid-May. So tomatoes, peppers, and eggplant go out around May 15. Corn and beans go in around May 20. Melons and cucumbers wait until late May or early June.

Here is a typical warm-season planting window for Zone 5:

| Crop       | Plant date (Zone 5) | Soil temp | Spacing |
|------------|---------------------|-----------|---------|
| Tomatoes   | May 15              | 60°F      | 24-36 in |
| Peppers    | May 15              | 60°F      | 18-24 in |
| Corn       | May 20              | 65°F      | 12 in    |
| Beans      | May 20              | 65°F      | 4 in     |
| Cucumbers  | May 25              | 70°F      | 12 in    |
| Melons     | May 25              | 70°F      | 36 in    |

If you want to get a jump on the season, use black plastic mulch to warm the soil, or start seeds indoors 6 to 8 weeks before the last frost. A soil thermometer costs about $10 to $15 and takes the guesswork out of planting.

## Succession planting: stagger for continuous harvest

Farmers don't plant everything at once. They use succession planting to have a steady supply of vegetables. For example, plant a row of lettuce every 2 weeks from early spring to late spring. The same goes for beans, radishes, and carrots. This way, you don't get a glut all at once.

For a homestead, plan to plant a new batch of a quick crop every 2 to 3 weeks. For example, in Zone 5, plant radishes on April 1, April 15, May 1, and so on. This gives you a continuous harvest from May through June.

## Fall planting: a second season

Many farmers also plant in late summer for a fall harvest. The key is to count backward from your first fall frost date. For cool-season crops, you want them to mature before the first hard freeze. For example, in Zone 5, first frost is around October 15. So you plant fall peas in early August, spinach in late August, and garlic in October (for next year's harvest).

Fall planting dates for Zone 5:

| Crop       | Plant date (fall) | Days to maturity |
|------------|-------------------|------------------|
| Peas       | August 1          | 60               |
| Spinach    | August 15         | 45               |
| Lettuce    | August 15         | 45               |
| Carrots    | July 15           | 70               |
| Garlic     | October 15        | 240 (overwinter) |

## Tools to help you plant on time

You don't need fancy equipment, but a few tools make a difference:

- Soil thermometer: $10 to $15, tells you when soil is ready
- Row cover: $20 to $30 for a 6x50 foot roll, protects from frost and pests
- Garden planner app or calendar: free or $5 to $10, helps you track dates
- A simple notebook: write down what you planted and when, so you learn your microclimate

## FAQ

**Can I plant before the last frost if I use row cover?**
Yes, you can plant cold-hardy crops 2 to 4 weeks before the last frost, and row cover can protect them from light frosts. For warm-season crops, row cover alone is not enough if soil is cold; you need to warm the soil first.

**What if I plant too early?**
Seeds may rot in cold, wet soil, and transplants may die from frost. You'll lose time and money. It's better to wait until soil and air temperatures are right.

**How do I know my soil temperature?**
Use a soil thermometer. Insert it 3 to 4 inches deep in the soil at mid-morning. Check a few spots in your garden, as south-facing slopes warm faster.

**Do farmers in warmer zones plant earlier?**
Yes. In Zone 8, you can plant peas in February and tomatoes in March. In Zone 9, you can plant year-round, but you'll have a summer lull when it's too hot for cool-season crops.

## The bottom line

The right planting time depends on your zone, your crop, and the soil temperature. Start with your last frost date, then plant cold-hardy crops 2 to 4 weeks before, and warm-season crops after the soil warms. Use a soil thermometer and a calendar to stay on track. If you're unsure, start small: plant a few cold-hardy crops early, and wait on the rest. You'll learn more from one season of observation than from any guide.
