The short answer: farms are disappearing because the economics no longer favor small and mid-sized operations. Between 2017 and 2022, the U.S. lost about 142,000 farms, a 7% drop, and the trend continues. The remaining farms are larger, more specialized, and often owned by corporations or investors. But this isn't inevitable. Understanding the forces at play helps you make informed choices, whether you're a gardener, homesteader, or simply a consumer who cares about where food comes from.

## The numbers: what's really happening

According to the USDA Census of Agriculture, the number of U.S. farms fell from 2.04 million in 2017 to 1.90 million in 2022. That's a loss of roughly 20,000 farms per year. The average farm size grew from 441 acres to 463 acres, meaning consolidation is real. Small farms (under 50 acres) saw the steepest decline, down 12% in that period. Meanwhile, farms over 2,000 acres increased in number.

Why? Three main reasons:

1. **Profit margins are razor thin.** Net farm income for small farms is often negative when you account for unpaid labor. The USDA reports that 43% of farms had negative net cash farm income in 2022. A family might gross $100,000 but spend $110,000 on inputs, equipment, and land payments.

2. **Debt and land prices.** Farm real estate values have doubled in many regions since 2010. In the Midwest, prime cropland sells for $10,000 to $15,000 per acre. A young farmer can't afford to buy land without inheriting it or taking on massive debt. Cash rent averages $150 to $250 per acre, which eats into any profit.

3. **Climate and weather volatility.** Droughts, floods, and heat waves have become more frequent. The USDA paid out record crop insurance claims, over $20 billion in 2023 alone, but that only covers a fraction of losses for small operations. Many farmers simply can't absorb another bad year.

## The economic squeeze: why small farms struggle

Small farms face a cost-price squeeze. Input costs (seed, fertilizer, fuel) have risen faster than crop prices. For example, fertilizer prices spiked 60% in 2022 and remain elevated. Meanwhile, commodity prices for corn and soybeans have fallen from their 2022 peaks. A corn farmer might pay $300 per acre for inputs but only get $4.50 per bushel, leaving little margin.

Labor is another issue. Finding reliable workers is hard, and wages are rising. The federal minimum wage is $7.25, but farm workers often earn $15 to $20 per hour. For a small operation, that's a huge expense. Many farmers work off-farm jobs to make ends meet; the USDA says 82% of farm operators have some off-farm income.

Regulations also add costs. Food safety rules, environmental compliance, and paperwork can be overwhelming. A small vegetable grower might spend $5,000 to $10,000 just to meet GAP (Good Agricultural Practices) certification for wholesale buyers.

## The human factor: who's leaving and why

The average age of a farmer is 58.1 years, and 40% of all farmers are over 65. As they retire, there aren't enough new farmers to replace them. Only 9% of farmers are under 35. The barriers to entry are high: land, equipment, and knowledge. Many young people lack the capital or the inheritance to start.

Succession planning is often absent. Only about 20% of farms have a formal succession plan. When a farmer retires or dies, the land is often sold to the highest bidder, usually a larger operation or a developer. That's why you see farmland being converted to housing or solar arrays.

Emotional stress is real. The suicide rate among farmers is higher than the national average. The financial pressure, isolation, and uncertainty take a toll. But this isn't just a rural issue; it affects everyone who eats.

## What you can do: practical steps for gardeners and homesteaders

You might not be able to stop corporate consolidation, but you can make a difference in your community. Here are concrete actions you can take this week:

- **Buy directly from farmers.** Join a CSA (Community Supported Agriculture). For $25 to $50 per week, you get a box of produce and support a farm directly. Search for local CSAs at LocalHarvest.org.
- **Shop at farmers markets.** The USDA reports that farmers markets have grown, but many small farmers rely on them for 50% or more of their income. Spend $20 at your local market instead of the grocery store.
- **Advocate for policy change.** Write to your representatives about the Farm Bill, which funds beginning farmer programs and conservation. Support policies that limit corporate farming and protect family farms.
- **Start small.** If you have land, consider growing for market. Even a quarter-acre can produce $10,000 in vegetables if you sell at premium prices. Use high-value crops like salad greens, heirloom tomatoes, and herbs.
- **Join a land link program.** Many states have programs that connect retiring farmers with new farmers. For example, the National Young Farmers Coalition has resources.
- **Support farm-to-school programs.** Encourage your local school district to buy from local farms. This creates a stable market for small growers.

## The role of agritourism: a lifeline for small farms

Agritourism is one way farms diversify income. Opening your farm for tours, pumpkin patches, or farm stays can bring in extra cash. For example, a corn maze can generate $10,000 to $30,000 per season, depending on location. A farm stay might earn $100 to $200 per night. This isn't a silver bullet, but it can make the difference between staying and selling.

If you're a homesteader, you can offer workshops on canning, cheese making, or raising chickens. Charge $50 per person for a two-hour class. With 10 participants, that's $500 for an afternoon.

## FAQ

**Q: Is the number of farms really declining?**
A: Yes. The USDA Census shows a 7% drop from 2017 to 2022, and the trend continues. Small farms are disappearing fastest.

**Q: Can I make a living on a small farm?**
A: It's tough. Many small farmers have off-farm jobs. But with direct sales, value-added products, and agritourism, some do. It requires hard work and business savvy.

**Q: What can I do if I want to start farming but can't afford land?**
A: Look into land linking programs, lease land, or start with a community garden. You can also farm on a small plot and expand as you build capital.

**Q: How does climate change affect farms?**
A: Extreme weather events like droughts and floods reduce yields and increase costs. Farmers are adapting with cover crops and drought-resistant varieties, but it's a constant challenge.

## The bottom line

We're losing farms because of economics, consolidation, and an aging farmer population. But every purchase you make is a vote. Support local farmers, advocate for policy change, and consider starting your own small operation. It won't happen overnight, but small actions add up. The future of farming depends on all of us.
