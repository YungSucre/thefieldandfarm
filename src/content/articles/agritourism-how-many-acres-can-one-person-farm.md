The short answer: one person can manage 1 to 5 acres of mixed vegetable production with hand tools and a walk-behind tractor, or up to 10 acres if you focus on grains or hay with larger equipment. But the real limit is not land, it is labor: a solo farmer typically works 40 to 60 hours a week during the season, and every crop has a specific time cost. Three factors set your ceiling: crop type, mechanization level, and market. Vegetables are labor intensive: you might spend 1,000 hours per acre on salad mix, but only 50 hours per acre on wheat. Mechanization shifts the balance: a BCS walk-behind tractor (around $3,500 new) can reduce tillage and cultivation time by half, while a compact tractor ($15,000 to $25,000 used) opens up hay and grain production. For intensive vegetables, 1 acre is a full-time job from April to October. For example, a 1-acre market garden with 30 beds of 100 feet each can yield $30,000 to $60,000 in gross sales, but you will spend 30 to 40 hours a week on planting, weeding, and harvesting. If you grow staple crops like potatoes or winter squash, you can handle 3 to 5 acres because these are less perishable and you can harvest in batches. Grains and hay are the most land-efficient for solo work: with a used tractor and a drill, one person can plant and harvest 10 acres of wheat, yielding about 400 bushels (worth $2,800 at $7 per bushel in 2026), but the profit margin is thin.

## Start Small: The 1-Acre Model

If you are new to farming, start with 1 acre of mixed vegetables. This size lets you learn soil management, pest control, and marketing without burnout. Here is a practical layout: divide the acre into 10 beds of 4 feet by 100 feet, with 30-inch paths. Plant high-value crops like tomatoes, peppers, and salad greens. Use a walk-behind tractor for tillage, a tilther for bed prep, and a drip irrigation kit ($200 to $400 for 1 acre). Plan for 20 to 30 varieties to spread risk. For example, plant 50 tomato plants (like 'Cherokee Purple' or 'Roma'), 100 pepper plants ('California Wonder'), and 200 feet of lettuce mix. Harvest 3 times a week, and sell at a local farmers market or through a 20-member CSA. This scale can gross $20,000 to $40,000, but expect to reinvest 30% in seeds, amendments, and tools.

## Scaling Up: When to Add Acres

Add acres only when you have systems in place. If you want to grow to 5 acres, invest in a compact tractor (40 to 50 horsepower, $20,000 to $30,000 used) and a grain drill ($2,000 to $4,000). This allows you to grow field crops like corn or soybeans, but note that commodity prices are low: corn at $4.50 per bushel means 5 acres yields only $2,800 gross. Instead, consider high-value field crops like dry beans ($1.50 per pound, 1,500 pounds per acre) or popcorn ($1 per pound, 2,000 pounds per acre). For hay, 5 acres of alfalfa can yield 5 tons per acre, selling at $200 per ton, but you need a baler (rent or buy used for $5,000). The key is to add land only when your labor and equipment can handle it: a solo farmer with a tractor can manage 5 acres of field crops, but 10 acres of vegetables will overwhelm you.

## Tools That Make Solo Farming Possible

Your toolset determines your acreage. Here is a realistic list for 2026, with prices:

- Walk-behind tractor (BCS or Grillo): $3,500 to $5,000 new, $2,000 used. Use for tilling, cultivating, and mowing.
- Tilther (for bed prep): $400 to $600. Saves hours of hand work.
- Drip irrigation kit: $200 to $400 per acre. Reduces watering time and weed pressure.
- BCS flail mower: $1,200 to $1,500. For cover crops and paths.
- Compact tractor (used): $15,000 to $25,000. Essential for grains and hay.
- Grain drill (used): $2,000 to $4,000. For planting small grains.

If you are on a budget, start with hand tools: a broadfork ($150), a wheel hoe ($200), and a seed drill attachment for your walk-behind ($300). These keep you under $1,000 and let you farm 1 acre effectively.

## Time Management for Solo Farmers

Farming alone means every hour counts. Here are practical tips:

- Plan your week on Sunday: list tasks for each day, prioritizing harvest and planting.
- Use season extension: hoophouses (a 20x100 foot hoop house costs $3,000 to $5,000) let you grow into early spring and late fall, spreading your income.
- Automate watering with timers on drip lines.
- Keep a field journal to track what works and what does not.
- Accept that you cannot do everything: skip crops that require daily picking, like strawberries, unless you have a pick-your-own operation.

## FAQ

**Can one person farm 100 acres?**
Only with heavy mechanization and a focus on grains or hay, and even then, you will work long hours during planting and harvest. A solo farmer with a 100-acre combine can manage 100 acres of wheat, but you need a $200,000 combine, which is not realistic for most homesteaders. For vegetables, 100 acres is impossible alone.

**How many acres do I need to make a living?**
For a full-time income of $40,000 net, you need 2 to 3 acres of intensive vegetables, or 10 to 20 acres of field crops, or a combination. Gross sales need to be $60,000 to $80,000, and you will keep 50% after expenses. Start with 1 acre and scale as you learn.

**What is the easiest crop for a solo farmer?**
Garlic is a great choice: plant in October, weed a few times in spring, harvest in July. One acre of garlic can yield 10,000 bulbs, selling at $2 each, but the initial seed cost is high ($1,000 per acre). Other low-labor crops include winter squash, potatoes, and hay.

**How many hours a day does a solo farmer work?**
During the season (April to October), expect 10 to 12 hours a day, 6 days a week. In winter, you will work 20 hours a week on planning and maintenance. It is not a 9-to-5 job, but it is rewarding.

## The Bottom Line

One person can farm 1 to 2 acres of vegetables, 5 acres of field crops, or 10 acres of grains and hay, depending on equipment and crop choice. Start with 1 acre of mixed vegetables, invest in a walk-behind tractor and drip irrigation, and expect to work 50 hours a week. Scale up only when you have systems and markets in place. The key is to match your land to your labor, not the other way around. Begin this week by mapping your site, testing your soil, and choosing 5 crops to grow next season.
