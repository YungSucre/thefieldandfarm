Onions take 90 to 120 days from seed, 60 to 80 days from sets, and 70 to 90 days from transplants, depending on variety and day length. The exact number depends on your growing zone and whether you planted short-day, intermediate-day, or long-day onions. Here is how to time it right and know when to pull them.

## Onion types and day length

Onions bulb based on day length, not just calendar days. Choose the right type for your latitude:

- **Short-day onions**: bulb when daylight reaches 10 to 12 hours. Best for zones 7 and warmer (southern U.S.). Plant in fall, harvest in late spring. Varieties: 'Red Creole', 'Texas SuperSweet', 'Yellow Granex'.
- **Intermediate-day onions**: bulb at 12 to 14 hours. Good for zones 5 and 6 (transitional areas). Plant in early spring or fall. Varieties: 'Candy', 'Superstar', 'Red Wing'.
- **Long-day onions**: bulb when daylight exceeds 14 to 16 hours. Best for zones 6 and cooler (northern U.S.). Plant in early spring, harvest in mid to late summer. Varieties: 'Walla Walla', 'Yellow Sweet Spanish', 'Red Burgermaster'.

If you plant the wrong type for your zone, you will get lots of tops but small bulbs. Check your zone and buy accordingly.

## Days to maturity by planting method

| Planting method | Days to harvest | Best time to plant |
|-----------------|-----------------|---------------------|
| Seeds | 90 to 120 days | 8 to 10 weeks before last frost indoors, or direct sow 4 weeks before last frost |
| Sets (small bulbs) | 60 to 80 days | 2 to 4 weeks before last frost (spring) or 6 to 8 weeks before first fall frost |
| Transplants (seedlings) | 70 to 90 days | 2 to 4 weeks before last frost |

Sets are the fastest but often bolt (go to seed) if they are large. Seeds give you more variety but take longer. Transplants are a good middle ground.

## Signs your onions are ready

Do not rely on the calendar alone. Look for these signs:

- **Tops fall over**: when about 50% of the green tops have flopped over naturally, that is the signal. The neck softens and the top bends. This usually happens 2 to 3 weeks before full maturity.
- **Bulb size**: feel the bulb at the soil surface. It should be firm and have a defined shape. If it still feels flat, wait.
- **Neck softness**: the neck (where leaves meet bulb) should be soft and pliable, not stiff.
- **Skin color**: the outer skin starts to dry and papery.

Once tops fall, stop watering and let the soil dry for a few days. Then lift the onions with a fork, not by pulling the tops, to avoid bruising.

## How to harvest and cure

1. **Lift gently**: use a garden fork to loosen soil under the bulbs. Do not pull by the tops.
2. **Leave tops on**: do not cut them off yet. Leave the roots and tops intact.
3. **Cure**: place onions in a warm, dry, well-ventilated spot (like a shed or garage) for 2 to 3 weeks. Spread them in a single layer on a wire rack or mesh screen. Avoid direct sun, which can scald them.
4. **Cut tops and roots**: after curing, cut the tops down to 1 inch and trim roots. Do not wash; just brush off soil.
5. **Store**: keep in a cool, dry place (40 to 50°F) with good air circulation. Use mesh bags or pantyhose. Properly cured onions can last 6 to 8 months.

## Regional timing examples

- **Zone 5 (e.g., Chicago)**: plant long-day sets in mid-April, harvest in early August (about 100 days).
- **Zone 7 (e.g., Atlanta)**: plant short-day sets in October, harvest in late May (about 90 days).
- **Zone 9 (e.g., Houston)**: plant short-day sets in November, harvest in May (about 100 days).

Check your local extension service for exact dates for your area.

## FAQ

**Can I harvest onions early?**
Yes, you can pull green onions (scallions) at any stage. If you want full bulbs, wait until tops fall. Early harvest gives smaller, milder onions.

**What if my onions bolt (send up a flower stalk)?**
Bolting stops bulb growth. Harvest immediately and use them within a few weeks; they will not store well. To prevent, avoid large sets and sudden temperature swings.

**How do I know if my onions are cured enough?**
The skin should be papery and the neck should be tight. Cut a test onion: if the neck is still moist, cure longer.

**Can I leave onions in the ground over winter?**
In mild climates (zones 7 and warmer), you can mulch and leave them, but they may rot in wet soil. Better to harvest and store.

## The bottom line

Plan for 90 to 120 days from seed, 60 to 80 from sets, and 70 to 90 from transplants. Match your variety to your day length, watch for tops falling over, and cure properly for long storage. With the right timing, you will have homegrown onions for months.
