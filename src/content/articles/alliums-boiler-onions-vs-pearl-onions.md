Boiler onions and pearl onions are both small, but they are not the same. Boiler onions are slightly larger, about 1 to 1.5 inches in diameter, and are typically used for pickling, roasting, or in stews where they hold their shape. Pearl onions are smaller, usually under 1 inch, and are prized for their sweet, mild flavor in dishes like creamed onions or as a garnish. If you want a versatile cooking onion that stands up to heat, choose boiler onions. If you want a delicate, sweet bite for elegant sides, choose pearl onions. Here is how to tell them apart, grow them, and use them.

## Key differences: size, flavor, and use

Boiler onions are simply immature or small varieties of standard bulb onions. They have a stronger, more pungent flavor than pearls, but they mellow when cooked. Pearl onions are usually a specific type, like 'Purplette' or 'Cipollini', that are naturally small and sweet. In the store, you will often find boiler onions in mesh bags labeled "boilers" or "pickling onions," while pearl onions come in small netted bags or frozen. For cooking,
![Boiler onions vs pearl onions](/images/articles/boiler-onions-vs-pearl-onions/section-2.jpg)
 boilers are better for long simmering; pearls are best added at the end or used raw in salads.

## Growing your own: planting and spacing

You can grow both from seed or sets. For boiler onions, plant sets in early spring as soon as the soil can be worked, about 4 to 6 weeks before the last frost. For pearl onions, start seeds indoors 8 to 10 weeks before the last frost, or sow directly in early spring. Spacing matters: boilers need 2 to 3 inches between plants, pearls only 1 to 2 inches. Plant seeds at 1/4 inch deep, sets at 1 inch deep. Both prefer full sun and well-drained soil with a pH of 6.0 to 7.0. In USDA zones 3 to 9, you can plant sets in March or April; in zones 8 to 10, plant in fall for a spring harvest.

## Harvesting and storing

Harvest boilers when the tops begin to yellow and fall over, usually in mid to late summer. Pull them and cure in a warm, dry place for 2 to 3 weeks. Pearl onions are often harvested earlier, when they reach 1/2 to 1 inch, but you can leave them to mature. For storage, boilers keep for several months in a cool, dry spot (32-40°F). Pearls are more perishable; use them within a few weeks or freeze them after blanching. If you grow 'Red Marble' or 'Purplette', they store reasonably well for a few months.

## Varieties to try

For boiler onions, look for 'Stuttgarter' (yellow, reliable), 'Red Wethersfield' (red, mild), or 'Ebenezer' (white, crisp). These are standard for pickling. For pearl onions, 'Purplette' (purple, sweet), 'Cipollini' (flat, Italian, very sweet), and 'White Pearl' (tiny, white) are excellent. In 2026, seed packets cost $3 to $5, and sets are $8 to $12 per pound. A 1-pound bag of boiler onions at the grocery store runs $3 to $5; pearl onions are pricier, at $5 to $7 for a 10-ounce net. Growing your own saves money, but pearl onions are slower to mature, so plan accordingly.

## Cooking with each type

Boiler onions are workhorses: roast them with root vegetables, add to beef stew, or pickle them for a tangy snack. Their stronger flavor stands up to bold seasonings. Pearl onions shine in cream sauces, glazed with butter and sugar, or skewered on kebabs. They are also great in cocktails like a Gibson. A simple tip: to peel pearl onions easily, blanch them in boiling water for 30 seconds, then plunge into ice water. The skins slip right off. For boilers, you can peel with a paring knife, but blanching works too.

## Cost and yield comparison

In 2026, expect yields of about 10 to 15 pounds per 10-foot row for boilers, and 5 to 8 pounds for pearls, because they are smaller. Seed costs are similar, but pearl onion seeds are often more expensive per ounce. If you are growing for pickling, boilers give you more volume for the effort. If you want gourmet sides, pearls are worth the extra care.

## FAQ

**Can I substitute boiler onions for pearl onions in a recipe?** Yes, but expect a stronger flavor and slightly larger pieces. Cut boilers into quarters if you need smaller bits. For creamed onions, boilers work, but they will be more savory.

**How do I know when to harvest pearl onions?** Harvest when the bulbs are 1/2 to 1 inch across, usually 70 to 90 days after planting. If you leave them longer, they get bigger but lose sweetness.

**Are boiler onions just immature regular onions?** Often yes. Growers thin their onion rows and sell the thinnings as boilers. But some varieties are bred for small size, like 'Stuttgarter'. So you can grow any onion and harvest early, but dedicated varieties give better results.

**Can I grow both in containers?** Yes. Use a pot at least 12 inches deep and 18 inches wide. Plant boilers 2 inches apart, pearls 1 inch apart. Water regularly, and fertilize with a balanced 10-10-10 every 3 weeks.

## The bottom line

Boiler onions and pearl onions are distinct in size, flavor, and best use. Boilers are larger, more pungent, and ideal for cooking and pickling. Pearls are smaller, sweeter, and best for delicate dishes. Grow both if you have space: boilers for bulk, pearls for gourmet touches. In the store, buy boilers for stews, pearls for special sides. Either way, you will add depth to your kitchen.

Start with a few sets of 'Stuttgarter' for boilers and a packet of 'Purplette' for pearls. Plant them this spring, and by late summer you will have a harvest that covers both needs. For immediate use, pick up a bag of each at the market and try them side by side in your favorite recipe. You will taste the difference.
