Butter and ghee both start with cream, but they are not interchangeable in the kitchen. Ghee is butter with the milk solids and water removed, which gives it a higher smoke point and a longer shelf life. Butter is best for baking and spreading, while ghee shines for high-heat cooking and for those avoiding dairy solids. Here is the practical breakdown, including how to make both and what they cost in 2026.

## What is the difference?

Butter is about 80% fat, 16% water, and 4% milk solids (protein and lactose). Ghee is 99% fat, with all water and milk solids removed. That removal changes everything: ghee has a smoke point of 485°F (252°C) versus butter's 350°F (177°C). ## Making butter at home

If you have a cream source, butter is simple. You need heavy cream (at least 36% fat) and a stand mixer, food processor, or a jar with a lid. Pour 2 cups of cold cream into the mixer and beat on medium-high. After 5 to 7 minutes, the cream will whip, then break into butter and buttermilk. Strain the liquid, then rinse the butter in ice water, kneading to remove excess buttermilk (which causes spoilage). Salt to taste if you like. Yield: about 1 cup of butter from 2 cups of cream. Cost: if you buy cream at $4 to $6 per pint, you are paying $8 to $12 per pound, which is more than store butter ($3 to $5 per pound). But if you have your own cow or goat, the cost is just your time.

## Making ghee from butter

Ghee is easier than butter, because you start with unsalted butter. Use 1 pound (4 sticks) of good quality unsalted butter. Melt it in a heavy saucepan over low heat. Let it simmer gently for 10 to 15 minutes. You will see foam rise, then subside. The milk solids will sink to the bottom and turn golden brown. When the foam is mostly gone and the butter is clear and golden, it is done. Strain through a cheesecloth or fine mesh into a glass jar. Let it cool, then cap. Yield: about 1.5 cups of ghee from 1 pound of butter. Cost: if you use store butter at $4 per pound, your ghee costs about $2.67 per cup. Store-bought ghee runs $8 to $12 per 8-ounce jar, so you save significantly by making it yourself.

## Smoke points and best uses

| Fat | Smoke point | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Butter | 350°F (177°C) | Baking, sautéing at low heat, spreading, finishing sauces |
| Ghee | 485°F (252°C) | Frying, roasting, high-heat searing, Indian cooking, sautéing at high heat |

Use butter when you want flavor and browning in baked goods, like cookies or pie crusts. Use ghee when you need to cook at high heat without burning, such as stir-frying or roasting vegetables at 425°F. Ghee also has a nutty, rich flavor that some prefer, but it does not replace butter in recipes that rely on water content, like certain frostings or emulsions.

## Storage and shelf life

Butter keeps in the fridge for 1 to 3 months, or frozen for up to 6 months. Ghee, because it is anhydrous, stays safe at room temperature for 6 to 8 months if kept in a sealed jar away from light. In the fridge, ghee can last a year or more. For homesteaders, ghee is the better long-term storage option, especially if you lack reliable refrigeration. Always use a clean, dry spoon to scoop ghee to avoid introducing moisture.

## Nutritional differences

Ghee has slightly more fat per tablespoon (14g vs 12g for butter) because the water is gone. It contains no lactose or casein, making it suitable for many people with dairy sensitivities. Butter has trace amounts of lactose and casein, which can bother those with severe allergies. Both are high in saturated fat, so use in moderation. Ghee also has a higher concentration of fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E, and K, since the milk solids are removed but the fat remains.

## Which should you choose?

For baking and everyday spreading, keep butter in your fridge. For frying, roasting, and long-term storage, make ghee. If you have excess cream from your dairy animals, making both is a smart way to use it. You can also make ghee directly from cream by simmering it longer, but starting with butter is more foolproof for beginners.

## FAQ

**Can I use ghee in place of butter in any recipe?**
No. Ghee has no water, so it changes the texture of baked goods. Use it for cooking, not for recipes that require creaming butter with sugar. For those, stick with butter.

**How do I know when ghee is done?**
When the foam subsides and the butter is clear and golden, with brown solids at the bottom, it is done. Do not let the solids burn black; that ruins the flavor.

**Does ghee need to be refrigerated?**
No, if stored in a sealed container in a cool, dark place. But refrigeration extends its life, so it is fine either way.

**Can I make ghee from salted butter?**
Yes, but the salt will concentrate, so it may taste saltier. Unsalted is better for a neutral flavor.

## The bottom line

Butter and ghee are both valuable in a homestead kitchen. Butter is for flavor and baking; ghee is for high-heat cooking and storage. Making ghee from butter is cheap and easy, and it saves you money over store-bought. Try a batch this weekend: melt a pound of butter, watch it clarify, and you will have a versatile fat that lasts months. For most households, keep a pound of butter in the fridge and a jar of ghee in the pantry, and you are set for any recipe you encounter.
