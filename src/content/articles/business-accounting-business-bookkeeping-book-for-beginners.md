If you're a gardener, homesteader, or small farmer, keeping accurate books is not just about taxes; it's about knowing whether your operation is profitable. This guide walks you through the basics of farm bookkeeping, from setting up your system to tracking income and expenses, with realistic tools and prices for 2026.

## Why bookkeeping matters for your farm

## Choose your bookkeeping method

You have three main options: paper ledger, spreadsheet, or software. For most beginners, a spreadsheet or entry-level software is the sweet spot.

- **Paper ledger**: A simple notebook or pre-printed ledger book costs $10-$20. It's cheap and straightforward, but hard to summarize and prone to errors.
- **Spreadsheet**: Use Excel, Google Sheets, or a template. Free or low-cost (Google Sheets is free). You can create columns for date, description, category, income, and expense. This is flexible and easy to customize.
- **Software**: Options like QuickBooks Online, FreshBooks, or Wave. Wave is free for basic accounting. QuickBooks starts around $30/month for the Simple Start plan. These automate calculations and generate reports, but have a learning curve.

For a small farm or homestead, start with a spreadsheet. It's free, you likely already have the tools, and you can upgrade later if needed.

## Set up your chart of accounts

A chart of accounts is a list of categories for your income and expenses. Keep it simple but specific to farming. Common categories include:

- **Income**: Crop sales, livestock sales, eggs, honey, value-added products (jams, soaps), agritourism, CSA shares, farmers market sales.
- **Expenses**: Seeds, plants, feed, fertilizer, soil amendments, tools, equipment, repairs, fuel, utilities, insurance, licenses, marketing, packaging, labor, veterinary, fencing, greenhouse supplies, irrigation.

You can start with 10-15 categories and add more as needed. For example, instead of just "Supplies," break out "Seeds" and "Feed" to see where your money goes.

## Track your income and expenses

Record every transaction as it happens. Use a simple log with columns: date, description, category, amount, and payment method. For cash sales, note the source (e.g., farmers market, CSA). Keep receipts in a folder or scan them with your phone.

- **Income**: Record the date, what you sold, quantity, and total amount. For example, "May 15, sold 20 dozen eggs at $5/dozen, $100."
- **Expenses**: Record the date, what you bought, and the amount. For example, "April 3, purchased 10 bags of organic fertilizer, $45."

Update your log weekly to avoid a backlog. Set aside 15 minutes each Sunday to enter the week's transactions.

## Separate business and personal finances

Open a separate bank account and credit card for your farm business. This makes tracking much easier and is essential for tax purposes. Many banks offer free business checking accounts with no minimum balance. Use the business account for all farm income and expenses. If you pay for something personally, reimburse yourself from the business account and record it.

## Handle cash and in-kind transactions

Cash sales are common at farmers markets. Keep a simple cash log: date, items sold, amount. At the end of the day, deposit cash into your business account. For barter or trade (e.g., trading vegetables for hay), record the fair market value of what you received and gave. This is taxable income, so track it.

## Track inventory and assets

Inventory includes seeds, feed, and products you have on hand. You don't need a complex system, but know what you have and its value. For assets like tractors, tools, and buildings, keep a list with purchase date and cost. This helps with depreciation for taxes. For example, a rototiller that cost $500 and lasts 5 years can be depreciated at $100 per year.

## Use bookkeeping to make decisions

At the end of each month or quarter, review your numbers. Calculate your net income (total income minus total expenses). Look at which categories are highest. Are you spending too much on feed? Is your egg income covering costs? Use this data to adjust prices, reduce expenses, or focus on profitable products.

For example, if you spent $200 on seeds but only sold $150 of vegetables from those seeds, you might need to raise prices or grow different varieties.

## Prepare for tax season

Good bookkeeping makes tax filing easier. You'll need to report income and expenses on Schedule F (Profit or Loss from Farming). Keep all receipts and records for at least 3 years. Consider consulting a tax professional who understands farming. They can help you with deductions like depreciation, home office, and vehicle use.

## Tools and resources

- **Spreadsheet templates**: Search for "farm income and expense spreadsheet" online. Many free options exist.
- **Receipt scanner apps**: Use your phone's camera or apps like CamScanner (free) to digitize receipts.
- **Accounting software**: Wave (free), QuickBooks ($30/month), or Xero ($12-$65/month).
- **Books**: "Farm Accounting" by the USDA or "The Small-Scale Poultry Flock" by Harvey Ussery (includes bookkeeping tips).

## FAQ

### Do I need a separate bank account for my farm?

Yes, it's highly recommended. It simplifies tracking and proves to the IRS that you're running a business. Many banks offer free business checking.

### What if I only sell at a farmers market for cash?

Keep a simple cash log. Record each sale with date, items, and amount. Deposit cash weekly. This is still business income and must be reported.

### Can I use a simple notebook instead of software?

Absolutely. A paper ledger works if you're disciplined. Just be consistent and keep receipts. You can transfer to a spreadsheet later.

### How often should I update my books?

At least weekly. Daily is ideal if you have many transactions. Set a regular time, like Sunday evening, to enter data.

## The bottom line

Bookkeeping doesn't have to be complicated. Start with a spreadsheet, separate your finances, and record every transaction. Review your numbers monthly to make informed decisions. With consistent effort, you'll know exactly where your farm stands financially, and tax time will be a breeze.
